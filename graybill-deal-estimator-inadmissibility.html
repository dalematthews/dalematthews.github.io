Graybill–Deal inadmissibility for every pair of sample sizes

A detailed reader’s edition of the limiting-Bayes proof

Theorem

Consider two independent normal samples

\[ X_{ij}\sim N(\mu,\sigma_i^2), \qquad i=1,2,\quad j=1,\ldots,n_i, \]

with a common unknown mean \(\mu\), arbitrary unknown positive variances, and \(n_1,n_2\ge2\). Let \(S_i^2\) be the usual unbiased sample variances. Under squared-error loss, the Graybill–Deal estimator

\[ \delta_{\mathrm{GD}} = \frac{(n_1/S_1^2)\bar X_1+(n_2/S_2^2)\bar X_2} {n_1/S_1^2+n_2/S_2^2} \]

is inadmissible for every fixed pair \((n_1,n_2)\).

Here “dominates” means that another estimator has no greater risk for every \((\mu,\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2)\) and strictly smaller risk for at least one such parameter point. An estimator is inadmissible precisely when a dominator exists.

The proof is nonconstructive: it proves that a dominating estimator exists for each fixed pair of sample sizes, but it does not give a formula for that estimator.

The one genuinely deep input is Lehmann–Casella, Theory of Point Estimation, Chapter 5, Section 7, Theorem 7.15 (often referenced as Theorem 5.7.15; printed page 382). Everything else is derived in the three companion modules listed below.

The logical implication chain is

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_{\mathrm{GD}}\text{ admissible} &\Longrightarrow r\text{ admissible in the reduced experiment}\\ &\Longrightarrow \text{finite-prior Bayes approximants exist}\\ &\Longrightarrow \text{a probability }\nu\text{ satisfies }(\star)\\ &\Longrightarrow\text{a derivative contradiction}. \end{aligned} \]

We prove the last implication for both unequal and equal sample sizes, so the first assertion must be false.

1. Reduction to estimating a variance ratio

Put

\[ v_i=\frac{\sigma_i^2}{n_i},\qquad \tau=v_1+v_2,\qquad \theta=\frac{v_1}{\tau}. \]

Then \(\tau>0\), \(0<\theta<1\), and conversely

\[ \sigma_1^2=n_1\tau\theta,\qquad \sigma_2^2=n_2\tau(1-\theta). \]

To check one-to-one explicitly, consider the map

\[ T:(0,\infty)^2\longrightarrow(0,\infty)\times(0,1),\qquad T(\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2)=(\tau,\theta). \]

By definition, \(T\) is one-to-one if

\[ T(\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2) =T(\widetilde\sigma_1^2,\widetilde\sigma_2^2) \]

implies

\[ (\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2) =(\widetilde\sigma_1^2,\widetilde\sigma_2^2). \]

Suppose the two variance pairs have the same image \((\tau,\theta)\). Applying the inverse formulas above to each pair gives

\[ \sigma_1^2=n_1\tau\theta=\widetilde\sigma_1^2, \qquad \sigma_2^2=n_2\tau(1-\theta)=\widetilde\sigma_2^2. \]

Thus the two variance pairs are equal, which proves directly from the definition that \(T\) is one-to-one. Moreover, every \((\tau,\theta)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,1)\) produces a positive variance pair through the inverse formulas, so \(T\) is in fact a bijective reparametrization.

Define

\[ D=\bar X_2-\bar X_1,\qquad Y_i=\frac{S_i^2}{n_i},\qquad r=\frac{Y_1}{Y_1+Y_2},\qquad q=\frac{D^2}{Y_1+Y_2}. \]

The commonly used definition of the two-sample Graybill–Deal estimator is the inverse-estimated-variance weighted average

\[ \delta_{\mathrm{GD}} = \frac{(n_1/S_1^2)\bar X_1+(n_2/S_2^2)\bar X_2} {n_1/S_1^2+n_2/S_2^2}. \]

Indeed, the variance of \(\bar X_i\) is \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_i)=\sigma_i^2/n_i\), which is estimated by \(S_i^2/n_i=Y_i\). Thus \(n_i/S_i^2=Y_i^{-1}\) is the corresponding estimated precision, and the definition can be written

\[ \delta_{\mathrm{GD}} = \frac{Y_1^{-1}\bar X_1+Y_2^{-1}\bar X_2} {Y_1^{-1}+Y_2^{-1}}. \]

The coefficient of \(\bar X_2\) in this weighted average is

\[ \frac{Y_2^{-1}}{Y_1^{-1}+Y_2^{-1}} =\frac{Y_1}{Y_1+Y_2}=r, \]

while the coefficient of \(\bar X_1\) is

\[ \frac{Y_1^{-1}}{Y_1^{-1}+Y_2^{-1}} =\frac{Y_2}{Y_1+Y_2}=1-r. \]

Consequently,

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_{\mathrm{GD}} &=(1-r)\bar X_1+r\bar X_2\\ &=\bar X_1+r(\bar X_2-\bar X_1)\\ &=\bar X_1+rD. \end{aligned} \]

The known-variance oracle is

\[ M=\bar X_1+\theta D. \]

If \(E=M-\mu\), elementary bivariate-normal calculations give

\[ E\perp(D,S_1^2,S_2^2),\qquad E[E]=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(E)=\tau\theta(1-\theta). \]

For the variance calculation, first use

\[ E=M-\mu =(1-\theta)(\bar X_1-\mu)+\theta(\bar X_2-\mu). \]

The two sample means are independent, with variances \(v_1\) and \(v_2\). Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(E) &=(1-\theta)^2v_1+\theta^2v_2\\ &=(1-\theta)^2\tau\theta +\theta^2\tau(1-\theta)\\ &=\tau\theta(1-\theta)\{(1-\theta)+\theta\}\\ &=\tau\theta(1-\theta). \end{aligned} \]

For any measurable weight \(\phi(r,q)\) of finite risk,

\[ \bar X_1+D\phi-\mu =E+D(\phi-\theta). \]

The cross term vanishes after squaring and taking expectations, so

\[ R_{\mu,\tau,\theta}(\bar X_1+D\phi) = \tau\theta(1-\theta) + \tau E_\theta\!\left[ \frac{D^2}{\tau}\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}^2 \right]. \tag{1} \]

Since \(D/\sqrt\tau\sim N(0,1)\), the rule

\[ Q_\theta(C) = E_\theta\!\left[ \frac{D^2}{\tau}\mathbf 1_{\{(r,q)\in C\}} \right] \tag{2} \]

defines a probability measure. Equation (1) becomes

\[ R_{\mu,\tau,\theta}(\bar X_1+D\phi) = \tau\theta(1-\theta) + \tau\int(\phi-\theta)^2\,dQ_\theta. \tag{3} \]

Therefore it is sufficient to show that the reduced rule

\[ \phi_0(r,q)=r \]

is inadmissible for estimating \(\theta\) under squared loss when \((r,q)\sim Q_\theta\). Any reduced dominator \(\phi\) lifts to the full-model dominator \(\bar X_1+D\phi\) through (3).

All independence and risk calculations in this section are expanded in Sections 1–7 of the raw-sample and density bridge.

2. Exact density of the reduced experiment

Set

\[ a=\frac{n_1-1}{2},\qquad b=\frac{n_2-1}{2},\qquad h=a+b,\qquad p=h+\frac32. \]

After dividing the scale variables by \(\tau\),

\[ G_1=\frac{Y_1}{\tau} \sim\operatorname{Gamma}\left(a,\text{ scale }\frac{\theta}{a}\right), \]

\[ G_2=\frac{Y_2}{\tau} \sim\operatorname{Gamma}\left(b,\text{ scale }\frac{1-\theta}{b}\right), \]

and

\[ W=\frac{D^2}{\tau}\sim\chi_1^2. \]

These three variables are independent. Weighting their joint law by \(W\), as in the definition of \(Q_\theta\), changes the \(\chi_1^2\) density into the \(\chi_3^2\) density.

Use

\[ G_1=rt,\qquad G_2=(1-r)t,\qquad W=qt. \]

The absolute Jacobian is \(t^2\). Integrating out \(t\) with

\[ \int_0^\infty t^{p-1}e^{-At}\,dt=\frac{\Gamma(p)}{A^p} \]

gives the exact density

\[ \boxed{ f_\theta^Q(r,q) = C_{a,b}r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2} \frac{\theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}} {B_\theta(r,q)^p} } \tag{4} \]

on \(0<r<1\), \(q>0\), where

\[ C_{a,b} = \frac{2^p a^a b^b\Gamma(p)} {\sqrt{2\pi}\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)} \]

and

\[ B_\theta(r,q) = 2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta). \tag{5} \]

The density is strictly positive for every observation and every \(0<\theta<1\). Its full derivation, including the crossed powers of \(\theta\), is in Sections 8–13 of the raw-sample and density bridge.

3. What admissibility would imply

Suppose, for contradiction, that the reduced rule \(r\) were admissible. Apply Lehmann–Casella Theorem 5.7.15 with

\[ \mathcal X=(0,1)\times(0,\infty),\qquad \Theta=(0,1),\qquad L(\theta,d)=(d-\theta)^2. \]

The theorem’s assumptions hold:

  1. Lebesgue measure on \(\mathcal X\) is sigma-finite.
  2. Equation (4) is a density relative to that measure and is everywhere positive.
  3. Squared loss is continuous and strictly convex in \(d\).
  4. \((d-\theta)^2\to\infty\) as \(|d|\to\infty\).

Moreover, \(r\) has finite reduced risk because \(0<r,\theta<1\) implies \((r-\theta)^2\le1\).

Consequently, there are finite-support priors \(\pi_j\) whose Bayes rules \(A_j(r,q)\) converge to \(r\) for Lebesgue-almost every \((r,q)\).

Under squared loss, a Bayes action is the posterior mean. Factoring (4) inside Bayes’ formula gives

\[ A_j(r,q) = \frac{\int\theta w(\theta)B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\pi_j(d\theta)} {\int w(\theta)B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\pi_j(d\theta)}, \tag{6} \]

where

\[ w(\theta)=\theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}. \]

Define the reweighted probability

\[ \nu_j(d\theta) = \frac{w(\theta)\pi_j(d\theta)} {\int w\,d\pi_j}. \tag{7} \]

Regarding \(\nu_j\) as probabilities on the compact interval \([0,1]\), pass to a weakly convergent subsequence

\[ \nu_j\Rightarrow\nu. \]

For fixed \(0<r<1\) and \(q\ge0\),

\[ B_\theta(r,q) \ge \min\{2ar,2b(1-r)\}>0. \]

Thus \(B_\theta^{-p}\) and \(\theta B_\theta^{-p}\) are bounded continuous functions of \(\theta\in[0,1]\). Weak convergence passes both integrals in (6) to the limit, and the limiting denominator is positive. Hence

\[ A_j(r,q)\longrightarrow A_\nu(r,q) := \frac{\int\theta B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta)} {\int B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta)}. \tag{8} \]

On the full-measure set supplied by Lehmann–Casella, the same sequence converges to \(r\). Therefore \(A_\nu(r,q)=r\) almost everywhere. Both sides are continuous in \((r,q)\), so equality holds everywhere for \(q>0\). Continuity as \(q\downarrow0\) then gives

\[ \boxed{ A_\nu(r,q)=r \quad\text{for every }0<r<1,\ q\ge0. } \tag{$\star$} \]

Possible endpoint atoms of \(\nu\) cause no problem because

\[ B_0(r,q)=2ar>0,\qquad B_1(r,q)=2b(1-r)>0. \]

Every step in this compactness argument, including the distinction between sample-space almost-everywhere convergence and parameter-space weak convergence, is expanded in the limiting-Bayes bridge.

4. Consequences of \((\star)\) on the boundary \(q=0\)

Put

\[ \lambda=p-1=h+\frac12 \]

and

\[ u_\theta(r) =ar(1-\theta)+b(1-r)\theta, \]

so that \(B_\theta(r,0)=2u_\theta(r)\). Define

\[ I(r)=E_\nu[u_\theta(r)^{-p}], \qquad F(r)=E_\nu[u_\theta(r)^{-\lambda}]. \]

The identity \(A_\nu(r,0)=r\) gives

\[ E_\nu[\theta u_\theta(r)^{-p}]=rI(r). \]

Since

\[ u_\theta(r)=ar(1-\theta)+b(1-r)\theta, \]

we obtain

\[ F(r)=hr(1-r)I(r). \tag{9} \]

Also

\[ \frac{\partial u_\theta(r)}{\partial r}=a-h\theta, \]

and therefore

\[ F'(r)=-\lambda(a-hr)I(r). \tag{10} \]

Dividing (10) by (9),

\[ \frac{F'(r)}{F(r)} =-\lambda \left\{\frac{a}{hr}-\frac{b}{h(1-r)}\right\}. \]

Solving this elementary ODE gives

\[ F(r) =C r^{-\lambda a/h}(1-r)^{-\lambda b/h}. \tag{11} \]

Now define

\[ r_0=\frac{b}{h},\qquad c=\frac{ab}{h},\qquad Z=a-h\theta. \]

At \(r=r_0\), \(u_\theta(r_0)=c\) independently of \(\theta\). Put \(r=r_0+ct\) and divide (11) by its value at \(r_0\). This yields

\[ \boxed{ E_\nu[(1+tZ)^{-\lambda}] =(1+at)^{-\lambda a/h}(1-bt)^{-\lambda b/h}. } \tag{12} \]

The identity holds for

\[ -\frac1a<t<\frac1b, \]

which is exactly the condition \(0<r_0+ct<1\). This interval also keeps \(1+tZ>0\) for every \(Z\in[-b,a]\).

The derivation of (9)–(12), including differentiation under the integral sign, is in Parts I–II of the ODE, moments, and contradictions module.

5. Unequal sample sizes

Assume \(a\ne b\), equivalently \(n_1\ne n_2\). Differentiating (12) three times at zero gives

\[ E[Z]=a-b, \tag{13} \]

\[ E[Z^2] = \frac{a^2-ab+b^2+\lambda(a-b)^2}{\lambda+1}, \tag{14} \]

and

\[ E[Z^3] = \frac{(a-b) \{2(a^2+b^2)+3\lambda(a^2-ab+b^2) +\lambda^2(a-b)^2\}} {(\lambda+1)(\lambda+2)}. \tag{15} \]

Because

\[ \theta=\frac{a-Z}{h},\qquad \theta-r_0=\frac{a-b-Z}{h},\qquad 1-\theta=\frac{b+Z}{h}, \]

substitution of (13)–(15) gives

\[ \boxed{ E_\nu[(\theta-r_0)\theta(1-\theta)] = \frac{2ab(a-b)(2h+1)} {h^3(2h+3)(2h+5)}. } \tag{16} \]

At \((r,q)=(r_0,0)\), equation (5) has the constant value

\[ B_\theta(r_0,0)=\frac{2ab}{h}=2c. \]

Differentiating the quotient (8) from the right in \(q\) gives

\[ \partial_{q+}A_\nu(r_0,0) = -\frac{p}{2c} E_\nu[(\theta-r_0)\theta(1-\theta)]. \]

Using (16),

\[ \boxed{ \partial_{q+}A_\nu(r_0,0) = -\frac{(a-b)(2h+1)} {2h^2(2h+5)} \ne0. } \tag{17} \]

But \((\star)\) says \(A_\nu(r_0,q)=r_0\), a constant function of \(q\), whose right derivative must be zero. This is a contradiction.

Parts III–V of the ODE, moments, and contradictions module derive every line of (13)–(17), including the polynomial expansion behind (16).

6. Equal sample sizes

It remains to consider \(a=b\), equivalently \(n_1=n_2\). The derivative in (17) then vanishes by symmetry.

Put

\[ Y=1-2\theta. \]

The transform (12) becomes

\[ E_\nu[(1+sY)^{-\lambda}] =(1-s^2)^{-\lambda/2}. \tag{18} \]

Comparing the coefficients of \(s^2\) and \(s^4\), with \(p=\lambda+1\), gives

\[ E[Y^2]=\frac1p,\qquad E[Y^4]=\frac3{p(p+2)}. \tag{19} \]

In these centered coordinates,

\[ B_\theta(r,q) = a+2a\left(r-\frac12\right)Y +\frac q4(1-Y^2). \tag{20} \]

Differentiate the numerator and denominator in (8), first in \(r\) and then from the right in \(q\), at \((r,q)=(1/2,0)\). Substitution of (19) gives

\[ \boxed{ \partial_{q+}\partial_r A_\nu(1/2,0) = \frac{p-1}{4a(p+2)}>0. } \tag{21} \]

But \((\star)\) says \(A_\nu(r,q)=r\). Its derivative in \(r\) is identically \(1\), whose derivative in \(q\) is zero. Equation (21) is the required contradiction.

Part VI of the ODE, moments, and contradictions module derives (18)–(21), including every quotient-rule term and the two moment substitutions.

7. Conclusion

For every \(n_1,n_2\ge2\), exactly one of the following holds:

  1. \(n_1\ne n_2\), in which case (17) contradicts \((\star)\);
  2. \(n_1=n_2\), in which case (21) contradicts \((\star)\).

Therefore the reduced rule \(r\) cannot be admissible. By the definition of inadmissibility, there exists a reduced rule \(\phi\) whose squared risk is no larger for every \(\theta\in(0,1)\) and strictly smaller for at least one \(\theta\). Equation (3) lifts it to the estimator

\[ \delta_\phi=\bar X_1+D\phi(r,q), \]

which dominates Graybill–Deal for all \(\mu\in\mathbb R\) and all positive variance pairs, with strict improvement somewhere. Hence Graybill–Deal is inadmissible.

The estimator \(\phi\) may depend on the fixed pair \((n_1,n_2)\); the theorem does not assert that the same rule works simultaneously for every choice of sample sizes.

9. Status and scope

The proof has been reconstructed independently in its probability, decision-theory, and analytic parts and checked symbolically. Because the conclusion is unusually strong and potentially resolves a longstanding question, independent review by a specialist in statistical decision theory remains prudent.

The theorem concerns:

  • exactly two independent normal populations;
  • a common unknown mean;
  • arbitrary positive population variances;
  • ordinary squared-error loss;
  • fixed sample sizes \(n_1,n_2\ge2\).

It does not cover sample size \(1\), where the usual unbiased sample variance is unavailable.

Reader expansion: from the raw samples to the reduced experiment

This note expands the probabilistic part of the universal limiting-Bayes argument for arbitrary sample sizes

\[ n_1,n_2\ge 2. \]

It starts from the raw normal samples and ends with the exact density

\[ \boxed{ f_\theta^Q(r,q)= C_{a,b}\, r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2} \frac{\theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}} {\{2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta +q\theta(1-\theta)\}^{a+b+3/2}}, } \tag{0.1} \]

where

\[ a=\frac{n_1-1}{2},\qquad b=\frac{n_2-1}{2} \tag{0.2} \]

and

\[ \boxed{ C_{a,b}= \frac{2^{a+b+3/2}a^a b^b\Gamma(a+b+3/2)} {\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}. } \tag{0.3} \]

For the smallest unequal pair \((n_1,n_2)=(2,3)\), this reduces to the constant \(C_{1/2,1}=8/\pi\).

Nothing below uses the limiting-Bayes theorem. The purpose is to make the statistical reduction, all independence assertions, and every normalization constant independently checkable.

1. The raw model and the sample-variance convention

We observe two independent normal samples

\[ X_{ij}\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(\mu,\sigma_i^2), \qquad j=1,\ldots,n_i,\quad i=1,2, \tag{1.1} \]

where

\[ \mu\in\mathbb R,\qquad \sigma_1^2>0,\qquad \sigma_2^2>0. \]

The two samples are independent of one another. Throughout, \(S_i^2\) is the usual unbiased sample variance:

\[ S_i^2=\frac1{n_i-1} \sum_{j=1}^{n_i}(X_{ij}-\bar X_i)^2. \tag{1.2} \]

Consequently, the standard normal-sample theorem gives

\[ U_i:=\frac{(n_i-1)S_i^2}{\sigma_i^2} \sim\chi^2_{n_i-1}. \tag{1.3} \]

This convention matters. If one instead defined a sample variance with denominator \(n_i\), the scaling factors below would change.

Put

\[ D=\bar X_2-\bar X_1,\qquad Y_i=\frac{S_i^2}{n_i}, \tag{1.4} \]

and, whenever \(Y_1+Y_2>0\),

\[ r=\frac{Y_1}{Y_1+Y_2},\qquad q=\frac{D^2}{Y_1+Y_2}. \tag{1.5} \]

Null-set convention

Since \(n_i-1>0\), a \(\chi^2_{n_i-1}\) variable is strictly positive almost surely. Equation (1.3) therefore implies

\[ \mathbb P(S_i^2=0)=0. \]

Thus \(Y_1,Y_2>0\) almost surely and

\[ 0<r<1 \]

almost surely. We will also see that \(D\sim N(0,\tau)\) with \(\tau>0\); hence \(\mathbb P(D=0)=0\), so

\[ q>0 \]

almost surely. We may define \(r\) and \(q\) arbitrarily on the null sets where their displayed formulas are undefined. Such definitions do not change any probability or risk.

The value \(q=0\), used later in the analytic contradiction, is therefore a boundary value reached by continuity, not an event having positive probability.

2. The unequal-size Graybill–Deal weight

The usual Graybill–Deal estimator weights each sample mean by the reciprocal of its estimated variance:

\[ \delta_{\mathrm{GD}} = \frac{(n_1/S_1^2)\bar X_1+(n_2/S_2^2)\bar X_2} {n_1/S_1^2+n_2/S_2^2}. \tag{2.1} \]

Since \(Y_i=S_i^2/n_i\), equation (2.1) is

\[ \delta_{\mathrm{GD}} = \frac{Y_1^{-1}\bar X_1+Y_2^{-1}\bar X_2} {Y_1^{-1}+Y_2^{-1}}. \]

The coefficient of \(\bar X_2\) is

\[ \frac{Y_2^{-1}}{Y_1^{-1}+Y_2^{-1}} =\frac{Y_1}{Y_1+Y_2} =r. \tag{2.2} \]

The coefficient of \(\bar X_1\) is \(1-r\). Therefore, for arbitrary possibly unequal sample sizes,

\[ \boxed{ \delta_{\mathrm{GD}} =(1-r)\bar X_1+r\bar X_2 =\bar X_1+rD. } \tag{2.3} \]

So Graybill–Deal uses the random quantity \(r\) as an estimate of an unknown ideal weight.

3. Reparametrizing the two variances

The variances of the sample means are

\[ v_1=\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_1)=\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}, \qquad v_2=\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_2)=\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}. \tag{3.1} \]

Define

\[ \tau=v_1+v_2,\qquad \theta=\frac{v_1}{v_1+v_2}=\frac{v_1}{\tau}. \tag{3.2} \]

Because \(v_1,v_2>0\),

\[ \tau>0,\qquad 0<\theta<1. \]

Conversely,

\[ v_1=\theta\tau,\qquad v_2=(1-\theta)\tau, \tag{3.3} \]

and hence

\[ \sigma_1^2=n_1\theta\tau,\qquad \sigma_2^2=n_2(1-\theta)\tau. \tag{3.4} \]

Thus \((\tau,\theta)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,1)\) is simply another one-to-one parametrization of the two positive variances:

  • \(\tau\) is their overall scale on the sample-mean scale;
  • \(\theta\) is the ratio governing the optimal weight.

Since the sample means are independent,

\[ D=\bar X_2-\bar X_1 \sim N(0,v_1+v_2) =N(0,\tau). \tag{3.5} \]

Therefore

\[ D_0=\frac D{\sqrt\tau}\sim N(0,1), \qquad W=D_0^2=\frac{D^2}{\tau}\sim\chi^2_1. \tag{3.6} \]

In particular,

\[ \mathbb E_\theta W=1. \tag{3.7} \]

4. The known-variance oracle

Define

\[ M=\bar X_1+\theta D =(1-\theta)\bar X_1+\theta\bar X_2. \tag{4.1} \]

This is the inverse-variance estimator that would be used if \(v_1,v_2\) were known. Indeed, the inverse-variance coefficient on \(\bar X_2\) is

\[ \frac{v_2^{-1}}{v_1^{-1}+v_2^{-1}} =\frac{v_1}{v_1+v_2} =\theta. \tag{4.2} \]

Let

\[ A=\bar X_1-\mu,\qquad B=\bar X_2-\mu. \]

Then \(A\sim N(0,v_1)\), \(B\sim N(0,v_2)\), and \(A\) and \(B\) are independent. Also

\[ D=B-A \]

and the oracle error is

\[ E=M-\mu=A+\theta(B-A)=(1-\theta)A+\theta B. \tag{4.3} \]

Its covariance with \(D\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}(E,D) &=\operatorname{Cov}((1-\theta)A+\theta B,\ B-A)\\ &=-(1-\theta)\operatorname{Var}(A) +\theta\operatorname{Var}(B)\\ &=-(1-\theta)v_1+\theta v_2. \end{aligned} \tag{4.4} \]

Using \(v_1=\theta\tau\) and \(v_2=(1-\theta)\tau\),

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(E,D) =-(1-\theta)\theta\tau +\theta(1-\theta)\tau =0. \tag{4.5} \]

The pair \((E,D)\) is jointly normal because it is a linear transformation of the jointly normal pair \((A,B)\). Jointly normal random variables with zero covariance are independent. Hence

\[ E\perp D. \tag{4.6} \]

Its variance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(E) &=(1-\theta)^2v_1+\theta^2v_2\\ &=(1-\theta)^2\theta\tau +\theta^2(1-\theta)\tau\\ &=\tau\theta(1-\theta)\{(1-\theta)+\theta\}\\ &=\tau\theta(1-\theta). \end{aligned} \tag{4.7} \]

For a normal sample, the sample mean is independent of the sample variance. Using this fact within each sample and independence between samples,

\[ (\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\perp(S_1^2,S_2^2). \tag{4.8} \]

Therefore \((E,D)\), which is a function of the two means, is independent of \((S_1^2,S_2^2)\). Combining this with \(E\perp D\) gives

\[ \boxed{ E\perp(D,S_1^2,S_2^2). } \tag{4.9} \]

One can verify the last regrouping directly from densities:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{E,D,S_1^2,S_2^2} &=f_{E,D}\,f_{S_1^2,S_2^2}\\ &=f_E\,f_D\,f_{S_1^2,S_2^2}\\ &=f_E\,f_{D,S_1^2,S_2^2}. \end{aligned} \]

The final factorization is precisely (4.9).

5. Exact risk decomposition

Let \(\phi=\phi(r,q)\) be any measurable real-valued weight for which the displayed risk is finite, and define

\[ \delta_\phi=\bar X_1+D\phi(r,q). \tag{5.1} \]

Since \(M=\bar X_1+\theta D\),

\[ \delta_\phi-\mu =E+D\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}. \tag{5.2} \]

Squaring,

\[ \begin{aligned} (\delta_\phi-\mu)^2 ={}&E^2 +2ED\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}\\ &+D^2\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}^2. \end{aligned} \tag{5.3} \]

The cross term has expectation zero. Indeed, \(D\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}\) is a function of \((D,S_1^2,S_2^2)\), whereas \(E\) is independent of that entire vector and has mean zero. Thus

\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ED\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}\right] =\mathbb E[E]\, \mathbb E\!\left[D\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}\right] =0. \]

Using (4.7) and \(D^2=\tau D_0^2\), we obtain

\[ \boxed{ R_{\mu,\tau,\theta}(\delta_\phi) = \tau\theta(1-\theta) +\tau\, \mathbb E_\theta\!\left[ D_0^2\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}^2 \right]. } \tag{5.4} \]

The first term is the oracle risk and is the same for every \(\phi\). All comparisons between estimators of the form (5.1) reduce to the second term. For Graybill–Deal, \(\phi(r,q)=r\).

6. The risk-weighted probability law \(Q_\theta\)

Let

\[ \mathcal Z=(0,1)\times(0,\infty), \]

the almost-sure range of \((r,q)\). For a Borel set \(C\subseteq\mathcal Z\), define

\[ Q_\theta(C) = \mathbb E_\theta\!\left[ D_0^2\,\mathbf 1_{\{(r,q)\in C\}} \right]. \tag{6.1} \]

This is a probability measure:

  1. it is nonnegative;

  2. countable additivity follows from monotone convergence;

  3. by (3.7),

    \[ Q_\theta(\mathcal Z)=\mathbb E_\theta[D_0^2]=1. \]

More generally, for every nonnegative measurable \(g\),

\[ \int_{\mathcal Z}g(r,q)\,Q_\theta(d r\,d q) = \mathbb E_\theta[D_0^2g(r,q)]. \tag{6.2} \]

This follows first for indicators by definition, then for nonnegative simple functions by linearity, and finally for arbitrary nonnegative measurable functions by monotone convergence.

Applying (6.2) to \(g(r,q)=\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}^2\), equation (5.4) becomes

\[ \boxed{ R_{\mu,\tau,\theta}(\delta_\phi) = \tau\theta(1-\theta) +\tau\int \{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}^2\,Q_\theta(d r\,d q). } \tag{6.3} \]

Thus the reduced problem is an ordinary decision problem:

  • parameter: \(\theta\in(0,1)\);
  • observation: \((r,q)\sim Q_\theta\);
  • action: the reported value \(\phi(r,q)\);
  • loss: squared error \((\phi-\theta)^2\).

This explains why \(Q_\theta\) is introduced: it absorbs the factor \(D_0^2\) already present in the original risk. It is not an arbitrary change of model.

Why reduced domination implies full domination

Suppose \(\psi(r,q)\) satisfies

\[ \int(\psi-\theta)^2\,dQ_\theta \le \int(r-\theta)^2\,dQ_\theta \qquad(0<\theta<1), \tag{6.4} \]

with strict inequality for at least one \(\theta\). Multiplying by \(\tau>0\) and adding the common oracle term in (6.3) shows that

\[ \bar X_1+D\psi(r,q) \]

dominates Graybill–Deal in the original normal problem. This holds for every \(\mu\) and \(\tau>0\). Equation (3.4) shows that all positive variance pairs are covered.

We only need to find a dominator inside the subclass (5.1). Allowing all possible estimators in the original problem cannot restore admissibility after a dominator has been found in this smaller subclass.

7. Removing the nuisance location and scale

The common mean \(\mu\) does not enter the law of \(D\), the sample variances, \(r\), or \(q\).

Define the normalized variables

\[ G_1=\frac{Y_1}{\tau},\qquad G_2=\frac{Y_2}{\tau},\qquad W=\frac{D^2}{\tau}=D_0^2. \tag{7.1} \]

Then

\[ r=\frac{G_1}{G_1+G_2}, \qquad q=\frac{W}{G_1+G_2}. \tag{7.2} \]

Therefore the distribution of \((r,q)\) is unchanged if we set \(\tau=1\). After division by \(\tau\), it depends only on \(\theta\).

This is the precise meaning of “normalize \(\tau=1\).” It does not assume special values of the original variances; it applies an invertible scale change to the variables.

8. The component laws for arbitrary \(n_1,n_2\)

Recall

\[ a=\frac{n_1-1}{2},\qquad b=\frac{n_2-1}{2}, \]

so \(n_1-1=2a\) and \(n_2-1=2b\). From (1.3),

\[ U_1\sim\chi^2_{2a}, \qquad U_2\sim\chi^2_{2b}. \tag{8.1} \]

Solving (1.3) for \(S_i^2\),

\[ S_1^2=\frac{\sigma_1^2}{2a}U_1, \qquad S_2^2=\frac{\sigma_2^2}{2b}U_2. \tag{8.2} \]

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} G_1 &=\frac{S_1^2}{n_1\tau} =\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1\tau}\frac{U_1}{2a} =\theta\frac{U_1}{2a}, \\[1ex] G_2 &=\frac{S_2^2}{n_2\tau} =\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2\tau}\frac{U_2}{2b} =(1-\theta)\frac{U_2}{2b}. \end{aligned} \tag{8.3} \]

A \(\chi^2_{2a}\) variable is

\[ \operatorname{Gamma}(a,\text{ scale }2). \]

Dividing it by \(2a\) gives

\[ \operatorname{Gamma}\left(a,\text{ scale }\frac1a\right). \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ G_1\sim\operatorname{Gamma} \left(a,\text{ scale }\frac{\theta}{a}\right), \qquad G_2\sim\operatorname{Gamma} \left(b,\text{ scale }\frac{1-\theta}{b}\right). } \tag{8.4} \]

For reference, a Gamma variable with shape \(\alpha\) and scale \(s\) has density

\[ \frac{x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x/s}} {\Gamma(\alpha)s^\alpha}, \qquad x>0. \tag{8.5} \]

Consequently,

\[ f_{G_1}(g_1) = \frac{a^a}{\Gamma(a)\theta^a} g_1^{a-1} \exp\!\left(-\frac{ag_1}{\theta}\right), \qquad g_1>0, \tag{8.6} \]

and

\[ f_{G_2}(g_2) = \frac{b^b}{\Gamma(b)(1-\theta)^b} g_2^{b-1} \exp\!\left(-\frac{bg_2}{1-\theta}\right), \qquad g_2>0. \tag{8.7} \]

From (3.6),

\[ W\sim\chi^2_1, \]

with density

\[ f_W(w) = \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}w^{-1/2}e^{-w/2}, \qquad w>0. \tag{8.8} \]

The variables \(G_1,G_2,W\) are mutually independent:

  • \(G_1\) and \(G_2\) are functions of different independent samples;
  • \(W\) is a function of the two sample means;
  • the vector of sample means is independent of the vector of sample variances in the normal model.

9. What the \(Q_\theta\) weighting does

It is convenient temporarily to extend the weighting defining \(Q_\theta\) to the full triple \((G_1,G_2,W)\), and then take the distribution of \((r,q)\).

The weight is \(W\), so the \(W\)-density becomes

\[ f_W^Q(w) =w f_W(w) =\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}w^{1/2}e^{-w/2}. \tag{9.1} \]

This is normalized because

\[ \int_0^\infty w f_W(w)\,dw=\mathbb E W=1. \]

It is also exactly the density of \(\chi^2_3\):

\[ \frac1{2^{3/2}\Gamma(3/2)}w^{3/2-1}e^{-w/2} = \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}w^{1/2}e^{-w/2}, \tag{9.2} \]

because \(\Gamma(3/2)=\sqrt\pi/2\). Thus the risk weighting has the simple effect

\[ \chi^2_1\longmapsto\chi^2_3. \]

Since \(W\) was independent of \((G_1,G_2)\), and the weight depends only on \(W\), the joint weighted density remains the product

\[ f_{G_1}(g_1)f_{G_2}(g_2)f_W^Q(w). \tag{9.3} \]

Substitution of (8.6), (8.7), and (9.1) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{a^a b^b} {\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b) \theta^a(1-\theta)^b} g_1^{a-1}g_2^{b-1}w^{1/2}\\ &\qquad{}\times \exp\!\left\{ -\frac{ag_1}{\theta} -\frac{bg_2}{1-\theta} -\frac w2 \right\}. \end{aligned} \tag{9.4} \]

10. The change of variables \((G_1,G_2,W)\mapsto(r,q,t)\)

Put

\[ t=G_1+G_2. \tag{10.1} \]

The inverse transformation is

\[ G_1=rt,\qquad G_2=(1-r)t,\qquad W=qt, \tag{10.2} \]

on the domain

\[ 0<r<1,\qquad q>0,\qquad t>0. \]

It is smooth and one-to-one between the positive domains. Using columns in the order \((r,q,t)\),

\[ \frac{\partial(G_1,G_2,W)}{\partial(r,q,t)} = \begin{pmatrix} t&0&r\\ -t&0&1-r\\ 0&t&q \end{pmatrix}. \tag{10.3} \]

Its determinant is \(-t^2\), so the absolute Jacobian is

\[ \left| \frac{\partial(G_1,G_2,W)}{\partial(r,q,t)} \right| =t^2. \tag{10.4} \]

The power factors in (9.4) become

\[ \begin{aligned} G_1^{a-1}G_2^{b-1}W^{1/2} &=(rt)^{a-1}\{(1-r)t\}^{b-1}(qt)^{1/2}\\ &=r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2} t^{a+b-3/2}. \end{aligned} \tag{10.5} \]

Multiplication by the Jacobian \(t^2\) changes the \(t\)-power to

\[ t^{a+b+1/2}. \tag{10.6} \]

Define

\[ p=a+b+\frac32. \tag{10.7} \]

Then \(a+b+1/2=p-1\), so the power is \(t^{p-1}\).

The exponent in (9.4) becomes

\[ -tA_\theta(r,q), \]

where

\[ A_\theta(r,q) =\frac{ar}{\theta} +\frac{b(1-r)}{1-\theta} +\frac q2. \tag{10.8} \]

Hence the joint weighted density of \((r,q,t)\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} & \frac{a^a b^b} {\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b) \theta^a(1-\theta)^b}\\ &\quad{}\times r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2} t^{p-1}e^{-tA_\theta(r,q)}. \end{aligned} \tag{10.9} \]

11. Integrating out \(t\)

Since \(A_\theta(r,q)>0\),

\[ \int_0^\infty t^{p-1}e^{-At}\,dt =\frac{\Gamma(p)}{A^p}. \tag{11.1} \]

This is the defining Gamma integral after the substitution \(u=At\). Integrating (10.9) over \(t\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} f_\theta^Q(r,q) ={}& \frac{a^a b^b\Gamma(p)} {\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b) \theta^a(1-\theta)^b}\\ &\quad{}\times r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2} A_\theta(r,q)^{-p}. \end{aligned} \tag{11.2} \]

Put \(A_\theta\) over the common denominator \(2\theta(1-\theta)\):

\[ A_\theta(r,q) = \frac{ 2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta +q\theta(1-\theta)} {2\theta(1-\theta)}. \tag{11.3} \]

Define

\[ B_\theta(r,q) = 2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta +q\theta(1-\theta). \tag{11.4} \]

Then

\[ A_\theta(r,q)^{-p} = \frac{2^p\theta^p(1-\theta)^p} {B_\theta(r,q)^p}. \tag{11.5} \]

Substituting into (11.2), the powers of \(\theta\) are

\[ \theta^{p-a} =\theta^{b+3/2} \tag{11.6} \]

and

\[ (1-\theta)^{p-b} =(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}. \tag{11.7} \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} f_\theta^Q(r,q) ={}& \frac{2^p a^a b^b\Gamma(p)} {\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)} r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2}\\ &\times \frac{\theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}} {\{2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta +q\theta(1-\theta)\}^{p}}, \end{aligned} } \tag{11.8} \]

where \(p=a+b+3/2\). This is exactly (0.1)–(0.3).

The “crossed” exponents

\[ \theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2} \]

are not typographical errors. They arise from subtracting the original \(\theta^{-a}(1-\theta)^{-b}\) Gamma normalizers from the factor \(\{\theta(1-\theta)\}^p\) created when \(A_\theta^{-p}\) is simplified.

12. Why the density is normalized and everywhere positive

There is no need to perform the difficult double integral in \(r\) and \(q\). Normalization follows from the construction:

  1. the densities in (8.6), (8.7), and (9.1) each integrate to one;
  2. their product (9.3) is therefore a probability density;
  3. the bijective change of variables (10.2), using the exact Jacobian (10.4), preserves total mass;
  4. integrating out \(t\) preserves the remaining total mass.

Thus (11.8) integrates to one.

It is strictly positive for every

\[ 0<\theta<1,\qquad 0<r<1,\qquad q>0. \]

Indeed every factor in its numerator is positive, while

\[ B_\theta(r,q) = 2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta +q\theta(1-\theta)>0. \]

The reduced experiment is consequently dominated by two-dimensional Lebesgue measure \(dr\,dq\), and its density is everywhere positive on the open observation space. These are the density facts needed for the limiting-Bayes complete-class theorem.

Also, if \(N\subseteq\mathcal Z\) is null for the ordinary distribution of \((r,q)\), then it remains null after risk weighting:

\[ Q_\theta(N) =\mathbb E_\theta[ W\mathbf 1_{\{(r,q)\in N\}}] =0. \]

Thus the null-set conventions made in Section 1 remain harmless under \(Q_\theta\).

13. Check at \((n_1,n_2)=(2,3)\): why the constant is \(8/\pi\)

For \(n_1=2,n_2=3\),

\[ a=\frac12,\qquad b=1,\qquad p=3. \]

The general constant becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} C_{1/2,1} &= \frac{2^3(1/2)^{1/2}1^1\Gamma(3)} {\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(1)}\\ &= \frac{8(1/\sqrt2)(2)} {\sqrt{2\pi}(\sqrt\pi)(1)}\\ &= \frac{8\sqrt2}{\sqrt2\,\pi}\\ &=\frac8\pi. \end{aligned} \tag{13.1} \]

Moreover,

\[ r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}=r^{-1/2}, \]

and

\[ B_\theta(r,q) =r(1-\theta)+2(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta). \]

Thus (11.8) reduces to

\[ \boxed{ f_\theta^Q(r,q) = \frac8\pi\frac{q^{1/2}}{\sqrt r}\, \frac{\theta^{5/2}(1-\theta)^2} {\{r(1-\theta)+2(1-r)\theta +q\theta(1-\theta)\}^3}. } \tag{13.2} \]

14. Compact map of the reduction

The probabilistic chain is

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{raw normal samples} &\longrightarrow (D,Y_1,Y_2)\\ &\longrightarrow (W,G_1,G_2)\\ &\xrightarrow{\text{weight by }W} Q_\theta\\ &\longrightarrow (r,q). \end{aligned} \]

At the risk level,

\[ \begin{aligned} R(\bar X_1+D\phi) &= \underbrace{\tau\theta(1-\theta)} _{\text{common oracle risk}}\\ &\quad+ \underbrace{\tau \int(\phi-\theta)^2\,dQ_\theta} _{\text{part depending on }\phi}. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore, proving that \(r\) is inadmissible for estimating \(\theta\) in the \(Q_\theta\)-experiment is sufficient to prove that the original Graybill–Deal estimator is inadmissible.

The limiting-Bayes bridge for arbitrary sample sizes

This note expands the decision-theoretic part of the universal proof for arbitrary \(n_1,n_2\ge2\). Its purpose is to explain carefully why admissibility would imply the existence of a probability measure \(\nu\) satisfying

\[ A_\nu(r,q) = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 \theta B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta)} {\displaystyle\int_0^1 B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta)} =r, \tag{1} \]

first for \(0<r<1\), \(q>0\), and then also at the boundary \(q=0\).

Here

\[ a=\frac{n_1-1}{2},\qquad b=\frac{n_2-1}{2},\qquad p=a+b+\frac32, \]

and

\[ B_\theta(r,q) =2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta). \]

The later moment calculations and the unequal/equal derivative contradictions are developed separately.

1. What is the reduced decision problem?

Put

\[ v_1=\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1},\qquad v_2=\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2},\qquad \tau=v_1+v_2,\qquad \theta=\frac{v_1}{\tau}. \]

Because both variances are positive,

\[ 0<\theta<1. \]

Thus the reduced parameter space is

\[ \Theta=(0,1). \]

Let

\[ D=\bar X_2-\bar X_1,\qquad Y_1=\frac{S_1^2}{n_1},\qquad Y_2=\frac{S_2^2}{n_2}, \]

and define the scale-free observations

\[ r=\frac{Y_1}{Y_1+Y_2},\qquad q=\frac{D^2}{Y_1+Y_2}. \]

Except on null events, \(0<r<1\) and \(q>0\). The reduced sample space is therefore

\[ \mathcal X=(0,1)\times(0,\infty). \]

The action is a real number \(d\in\mathbb R\), interpreted as an estimate of \(\theta\), and the loss is

\[ L(\theta,d)=(d-\theta)^2. \]

The Graybill–Deal weight is the reduced rule

\[ d(r,q)=r. \]

Why inadmissibility here is enough

Gaussian regression gives

\[ \bar X_1-\mu=-\theta D+E, \]

where \(E\) is independent of \((D,S_1^2,S_2^2)\) and

\[ \operatorname{Var}(E)=\tau\theta(1-\theta). \]

Consequently, if \(\phi(r,q)\) is any reduced rule, the corresponding estimator of the common mean,

\[ \delta_\phi=\bar X_1+D\phi(r,q), \]

has risk

\[ R_{\mu,\tau,\theta}(\delta_\phi) = \tau\theta(1-\theta) + \tau E_\theta\!\left[ \frac{D^2}{\tau}\{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}^2 \right]. \tag{2} \]

Define the risk-weighted probability law

\[ Q_\theta(A) = E_\theta\!\left[ \frac{D^2}{\tau}\, 1_{\{(r,q)\in A\}} \right]. \tag{3} \]

It is a probability law because \(D/\sqrt\tau\sim N(0,1)\) and hence \(E_\theta(D^2/\tau)=1\). Equation (2) becomes

\[ R_{\mu,\tau,\theta}(\delta_\phi) = \tau\theta(1-\theta) + \tau\int_{\mathcal X} \{\phi(r,q)-\theta\}^2\,Q_\theta(d r\,d q). \tag{4} \]

Therefore, if some \(\phi\) dominates \(r\) in the reduced problem, then \(\delta_\phi\) dominates Graybill–Deal in the original problem. A strict improvement at one \(\theta\) remains strict after multiplication by every \(\tau>0\), and neither side depends on \(\mu\).

The contrapositive is what we use:

If Graybill–Deal were admissible in the original problem, then \(r\) would have to be admissible in the reduced problem.

This implication does not claim that every estimator of the common mean has the form \(\delta_\phi\). It only observes that a dominator found in this subclass would already disprove admissibility in the full class.

2. The reduced density and the part that matters for Bayes’ formula

Relative to two-dimensional Lebesgue measure \(d r\,d q\) on \(\mathcal X\), the risk-weighted law \(Q_\theta\) has density

\[ f_\theta^Q(r,q) = C_{a,b}r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2} \frac{\theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}} {\{2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta +q\theta(1-\theta)\}^{p}}, \tag{5} \]

where

\[ C_{a,b} =\frac{2^p a^a b^b\Gamma(p)} {\sqrt{2\pi}\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}>0. \]

For later convenience define

\[ c(r,q)=C_{a,b}r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2}, \qquad w(\theta)=\theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}, \tag{6} \]

and

\[ B_\theta(r,q) = 2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta). \tag{7} \]

Then simply

\[ f_\theta^Q(r,q)=c(r,q)w(\theta)B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}. \tag{8} \]

For every \((r,q)\in\mathcal X\) and \(\theta\in(0,1)\), all factors in (5) are strictly positive. Thus

\[ f_\theta^Q(r,q)>0. \tag{9} \]

This positivity is an important hypothesis of the complete-class theorem.

3. The exact theorem used from Lehmann–Casella

The relevant result is Theorem 7.15 in Chapter 5, Section 7 of Lehmann and Casella, Theory of Point Estimation, second edition (often referenced as Theorem 5.7.15; printed page 382, equation (5.7.12)). In the notation used here, it says:

Suppose that the experiment has a density \(f_\theta(x)\) relative to a sigma-finite measure \(m\), with \(f_\theta(x)>0\) for every sample point \(x\) and every parameter value \(\theta\). Suppose also that \(L(\theta,d)\) is continuous, strictly convex in \(d\), and tends to infinity as \(|d|\to\infty\). Then every admissible rule \(\delta\) is the \(m\)-almost-everywhere limit of Bayes rules for a sequence of priors having finite support.

Here the dominating measure \(m\) is two-dimensional Lebesgue measure on \(\mathcal X\). It is sigma-finite; for example,

\[ \mathcal X = \bigcup_{k=2}^\infty \left[\frac1k,1-\frac1k\right]\times \left[\frac1k,k\right], \]

and each rectangle has finite Lebesgue measure.

The remaining hypotheses are checked directly:

  1. Equation (9) gives strict positivity of the density.

  2. \((d-\theta)^2\) is continuous in \((\theta,d)\).

  3. Its second derivative with respect to \(d\) is \(2>0\), so it is strictly convex in \(d\).

  4. For every fixed \(\theta\),

    \[ (d-\theta)^2\longrightarrow\infty \quad\text{as }|d|\longrightarrow\infty. \]

The candidate rule also has finite risk: since \(0<r,\theta<1\), \((r-\theta)^2\le1\), and \(Q_\theta\) is a probability law.

We deliberately take the action space to be \(\mathbb R\), exactly as in the theorem’s coercivity condition. Although projection onto \([0,1]\) can only reduce squared error when \(\theta\in(0,1)\), no compact-action modification of the theorem is needed.

Assume now, for contradiction, that \(r\) is admissible in the reduced problem. The theorem supplies priors

\[ \pi_j=\sum_{k=1}^{N_j}\alpha_{jk}\delta_{\theta_{jk}}, \qquad \alpha_{jk}>0,\quad \sum_k\alpha_{jk}=1,\quad 0<\theta_{jk}<1, \tag{10} \]

whose Bayes rules, denoted \(A_j(r,q)\), satisfy

\[ A_j(r,q)\longrightarrow r \quad\text{for Lebesgue-almost every }(r,q)\in\mathcal X. \tag{11} \]

The phrase “almost every” in (11) refers to the sample-space Lebesgue measure \(d r\,d q\), not to a measure on the parameter space.

4. Computing each finite-prior Bayes rule

Under squared loss, the Bayes action is the posterior mean of \(\theta\). This follows by completing the square. If the posterior has mean \(m\), then

\[ E[(d-\theta)^2\mid r,q] = (d-m)^2+\operatorname{Var}(\theta\mid r,q), \tag{12} \]

which is uniquely minimized at \(d=m\).

For the finite prior (10), Bayes’ formula gives

\[ A_j(r,q) = \frac{\displaystyle \sum_k\theta_{jk}f_{\theta_{jk}}^Q(r,q)\alpha_{jk}} {\displaystyle \sum_k f_{\theta_{jk}}^Q(r,q)\alpha_{jk}}. \tag{13} \]

Substitute (8). The data-only factor \(c(r,q)\) cancels from numerator and denominator:

\[ A_j(r,q) = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 \theta\,w(\theta)B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\pi_j(d\theta)} {\displaystyle\int_0^1 w(\theta)B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\pi_j(d\theta)}. \tag{14} \]

This is the origin of the reweighting in the short proof.

5. Reweighting the priors

Let

\[ Z_j=\int_0^1 w(\theta)\,\pi_j(d\theta). \tag{15} \]

Every support point of \(\pi_j\) lies in \((0,1)\), and \(w(\theta)>0\) there. Therefore

\[ 0<Z_j<\infty. \tag{16} \]

Define a new probability measure

\[ \nu_j(d\theta) = \frac{w(\theta)}{Z_j}\,\pi_j(d\theta). \tag{17} \]

Indeed,

\[ \int_0^1\nu_j(d\theta) = \frac1{Z_j}\int_0^1w(\theta)\,\pi_j(d\theta)=1. \]

Multiplying both numerator and denominator of (14) by \(1/Z_j\) gives

\[ A_j(r,q) = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 \theta B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu_j(d\theta)} {\displaystyle\int_0^1 B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu_j(d\theta)}. \tag{18} \]

The numbers \(Z_j\) are allowed to approach zero. That causes no problem: each \(\nu_j\) is still an ordinary probability measure, and all later arguments concern \(\nu_j\), not the magnitude of \(Z_j\).

6. Taking a weakly convergent subsequence

We regard every \(\nu_j\) as a probability measure on the closed interval \([0,1]\). The space \([0,1]\) is compact. The standard sequential compactness theorem for probability measures on a compact metric space (equivalently, the compact case of Prokhorov’s theorem) gives a subsequence, which we continue to denote by \(\nu_j\), and a probability measure \(\nu\) on \([0,1]\) such that

\[ \nu_j\Rightarrow\nu. \tag{19} \]

By definition of weak convergence, (19) means that

\[ \int_0^1 g(\theta)\,\nu_j(d\theta) \longrightarrow \int_0^1 g(\theta)\,\nu(d\theta) \tag{20} \]

for every bounded continuous function \(g:[0,1]\to\mathbb R\).

Although each \(\nu_j\) is supported inside \((0,1)\), the limit \(\nu\) may put mass at \(0\) or \(1\). For example, \(\delta_{1/j}\Rightarrow\delta_0\). This is why the closed interval is used.

7. Why the posterior ratios converge pointwise

Fix any \(0<r<1\) and \(q\geq0\). As a function of \(\theta\in[0,1]\), \(B_\theta(r,q)\) is continuous. Moreover,

\[ B_\theta(r,q) \geq 2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta \geq \min\{2ar,\,2b(1-r)\}>0. \tag{21} \]

The first inequality uses \(q\theta(1-\theta)\geq0\). The second holds because the middle expression is the linear interpolation between \(2ar\) and \(2b(1-r)\).

It follows from (21) that both functions

\[ \theta\longmapsto B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}, \qquad \theta\longmapsto\theta B_\theta(r,q)^{-p} \tag{22} \]

are bounded and continuous on \([0,1]\). Apply (20) to each one:

\[ \int B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu_j(d\theta) \longrightarrow \int B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta), \tag{23} \]

and

\[ \int\theta B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu_j(d\theta) \longrightarrow \int\theta B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta). \tag{24} \]

The limiting denominator in (23) is strictly positive. The integrand is strictly positive for every \(\theta\), and \(\nu\) has total mass one. Therefore elementary convergence of quotients gives, at every fixed \(0<r<1\), \(q\geq0\),

\[ A_j(r,q)\longrightarrow A_\nu(r,q), \tag{25} \]

where \(A_\nu\) is the ratio in (1).

Notice that (25) is pointwise convergence produced by weak convergence, whereas (11) is almost-everywhere convergence produced by the Lehmann–Casella theorem.

8. Why the two limits must agree

Let \(E\subset\mathcal X\) be the full-Lebesgue-measure set on which (11) holds. Passing to a subsequence does not destroy convergence on \(E\). Thus, for every \((r,q)\in E\), the same sequence \(A_j(r,q)\) has the two limits

\[ A_j(r,q)\longrightarrow r \]

by (11), and

\[ A_j(r,q)\longrightarrow A_\nu(r,q) \]

by (25). A real sequence has at most one limit, so

\[ A_\nu(r,q)=r \quad\text{for Lebesgue-almost every }(r,q)\in\mathcal X. \tag{26} \]

We next upgrade (26) to equality everywhere in \(\mathcal X\).

The numerator and denominator in (1) are continuous functions of \((r,q)\) on \(\mathcal X\). One way to verify this carefully is to fix \((r_0,q_0)\in\mathcal X\) and choose a small neighborhood in which

\[ \varepsilon\leq r\leq1-\varepsilon, \qquad 0\leq q\leq M \]

for some \(\varepsilon>0\) and finite \(M\). Inequality (21) then gives the uniform lower bound \(B_\theta(r,q)\geq2\min(a,b)\varepsilon>0\) for every \(\theta\in[0,1]\). The two integrands are jointly continuous and uniformly bounded there, so dominated convergence proves continuity of their integrals. The denominator stays positive, hence \(A_\nu\) is continuous. The function \((r,q)\mapsto r\) is also continuous.

If two continuous functions differ at one point of the open set \(\mathcal X\), then, by continuity, they differ throughout some open ball around that point. Every nonempty open ball has positive Lebesgue measure, contradicting (26). Therefore

\[ A_\nu(r,q)=r \quad\text{for every }0<r<1,\ q>0. \tag{27} \]

No analytic-continuation theorem and no diagonal-subsequence argument is being used here.

9. Extending the identity to \(q=0\)

The statistical sample space has \(q>0\). At \(q=0\), the density (5) contains the factor \(q^{1/2}\) and vanishes. Thus \(A_\nu(r,0)\) should not be described as an ordinary posterior mean conditional on observing \(q=0\). It is instead the continuous boundary value of the ratio in (1).

Fix \(0<r<1\). Inequality (21) remains true for every \(q\geq0\). Therefore, as \(q\downarrow0\), dominated convergence gives

\[ \int B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta) \longrightarrow \int B_\theta(r,0)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta) \]

and the analogous convergence for the numerator. The limiting denominator is positive, so

\[ A_\nu(r,q)\longrightarrow A_\nu(r,0). \tag{28} \]

Taking \(q\downarrow0\) in (27) now gives

\[ A_\nu(r,0)=r \quad\text{for every }0<r<1. \tag{29} \]

This boundary identity is what permits the later \(q=0\) moment calculation and the right derivative in \(q\).

10. Why endpoint atoms of \(\nu\) are harmless

The limit measure \(\nu\) may have atoms at \(\theta=0\) or \(\theta=1\), even though these points are not members of the original parameter space. Direct substitution into (7) gives

\[ B_0(r,q)=2ar, \qquad B_1(r,q)=2b(1-r). \tag{30} \]

For \(0<r<1\), both numbers are strictly positive and do not depend on \(q\). Hence an atom at \(0\) contributes the finite quantities

\[ (2ar)^{-p}\nu(\{0\}) \quad\text{to the denominator},\qquad 0 \quad\text{to the numerator}, \]

and an atom at \(1\) contributes

\[ \{2b(1-r)\}^{-p}\nu(\{1\}) \]

to both numerator and denominator. There is no division by zero or divergent integral.

The same observation justifies taking the right derivative in \(q\) at \(q=0\): the \(q\)-dependent factor is \(\theta(1-\theta)\), which equals zero at both endpoints.

11. What has, and has not, been proved at this stage

Assuming reduced admissibility, the Lehmann–Casella theorem and the elementary limiting arguments above produce a probability measure \(\nu\) on \([0,1]\) satisfying

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^1 \theta \{2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta)\}^{-p} \,\nu(d\theta)} {\displaystyle\int_0^1 \{2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta)\}^{-p} \,\nu(d\theta)} =r } \tag{31} \]

for every \(0<r<1\) and every \(q\geq0\).

The remaining proof shows that no probability measure \(\nu\) can satisfy (31). When \(a\ne b\), its restriction to \(q=0\) forces moments making the right \(q\)-derivative nonzero at \(r=b/(a+b)\). When \(a=b\), that first derivative vanishes by symmetry, but a mixed \(r,q\)-derivative is strictly positive. Both conclusions contradict (31).

12. Subtle points worth checking independently

  1. The complete-class theorem is the deep input.
    Everything after its application is elementary weak convergence, continuity, and Bayes’ formula. The theorem proves existence of finite-prior approximants; it does not construct a dominating estimator.

  2. Use the action space \(\mathbb R\).
    This matches the theorem as printed. The finite-prior Bayes actions automatically lie in \((0,1)\) because they are posterior means of parameters in \((0,1)\).

  3. The dominating measure in the theorem and the limiting prior are different objects.
    The theorem’s measure is Lebesgue measure on the sample space. The symbol \(\nu\) in (31) denotes a probability measure on the compactified parameter space.

  4. The reweighting constants may tend to zero.
    Only their strict positivity for each finite \(j\) is needed.

  5. The limiting measure may live partly or entirely at the endpoints.
    Formula (30) shows that this causes no singularity.

  6. \(q=0\) is a boundary, not a positive-probability observation.
    The identity there follows from continuity of the ratio after equality has first been established for every \(q>0\).

  7. The density calculation and the Gaussian risk reduction are separate load-bearing inputs.
    The decision-theoretic bridge is correct provided equations (4) and (5) have been independently verified.

The universal analytic contradiction, in reader-level detail

This note expands the final analytic part of the proposed nonconstructive proof that the Graybill–Deal estimator is inadmissible for every pair \(n_1,n_2\geq2\).

It begins after the limiting-Bayes theorem has been applied. That decision-theoretic argument is assumed to have produced a probability measure \(\nu\) on \([0,1]\) satisfying the identity below. Everything in this note is then elementary calculus and algebra. In particular, all expectations are expectations under the single probability measure \(\nu\).

Put

\[ a=\frac{n_1-1}{2},\qquad b=\frac{n_2-1}{2},\qquad h=a+b, \tag{1} \]

and

\[ p=h+\frac32,\qquad \lambda=p-1=h+\frac12. \tag{2} \]

Because \(n_1,n_2\geq2\), both \(a\) and \(b\) are strictly positive. The identity forced by the assumed admissibility is

\[ A_\nu(r,q) := \frac{\displaystyle \int_0^1\theta B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta)} {\displaystyle \int_0^1 B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\,\nu(d\theta)} =r \tag{3} \]

for every \(0<r<1\) and \(q\geq0\), where

\[ B_\theta(r,q) =2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta). \tag{4} \]

The goal is to show that no probability measure \(\nu\) can satisfy (3).

There is no hidden problem caused by atoms of \(\nu\) at \(0\) or \(1\). For \(0<r<1\) and \(q\geq0\),

\[ B_\theta(r,q) \geq \min\{2ar,\,2b(1-r)\}>0, \tag{5} \]

because the first two terms in (4) form an affine interpolation between \(2ar\) and \(2b(1-r)\), and the \(q\)-term is nonnegative. Thus all the integrands used below are bounded when \((r,q)\) stays in a sufficiently small neighborhood of a point under consideration. Differentiation under the integral sign follows directly from dominated convergence.

Whenever a derivative in \(q\) is evaluated at \(q=0\), it is formally the right derivative because the statistical coordinate has \(q\ge0\). Equivalently, at each interior \(r\) the denominator in (5) remains positive for all sufficiently small negative \(q\), so the displayed integral formula has a local smooth extension across \(q=0\).

Part I. The \(q=0\) differential equation for general \(a,b\)

1. Remove the irrelevant factor \(2\)

At \(q=0\), define

\[ u_\theta(r) =ar(1-\theta)+b(1-r)\theta. \tag{6} \]

Then \(B_\theta(r,0)=2u_\theta(r)\). The factor \(2^{-p}\) cancels between the numerator and denominator of (3), so

\[ \frac{E_\nu[\theta u_\theta(r)^{-p}]} {E_\nu[u_\theta(r)^{-p}]} =r. \tag{7} \]

Define

\[ I(r)=E_\nu[u_\theta(r)^{-p}] \tag{8} \]

and

\[ F(r)=E_\nu[u_\theta(r)^{-\lambda}]. \tag{9} \]

The reason for choosing the exponent \(-\lambda=-(p-1)\) is that

\[ u_\theta(r)^{-\lambda} =u_\theta(r)\,u_\theta(r)^{-p}. \tag{10} \]

Equation (7) is precisely

\[ E_\nu[\theta u_\theta(r)^{-p}]=rI(r). \tag{11} \]

Therefore

\[ E_\nu[(1-\theta)u_\theta(r)^{-p}] =I(r)-rI(r) =(1-r)I(r). \tag{12} \]

Now multiply out (10), using the definition (6):

\[ \begin{aligned} F(r) &=E_\nu[u_\theta(r)u_\theta(r)^{-p}]\\ &=arE_\nu[(1-\theta)u_\theta(r)^{-p}] +b(1-r)E_\nu[\theta u_\theta(r)^{-p}]\\ &=ar(1-r)I(r)+b(1-r)rI(r)\\ &=(a+b)r(1-r)I(r)\\ &=hr(1-r)I(r). \end{aligned} \tag{13} \]

This is the first relation between \(F\) and \(I\).

2. Differentiate \(F\)

The derivative of (6) with respect to \(r\) is

\[ \frac{\partial u_\theta(r)}{\partial r} =a(1-\theta)-b\theta =a-(a+b)\theta =a-h\theta. \tag{14} \]

Consequently,

\[ \begin{aligned} F'(r) &=-\lambda E_\nu[(a-h\theta)u_\theta(r)^{-\lambda-1}]\\ &=-\lambda E_\nu[(a-h\theta)u_\theta(r)^{-p}]\\ &=-\lambda \left\{aI(r)-hE_\nu[\theta u_\theta(r)^{-p}]\right\}\\ &=-\lambda(a-hr)I(r), \end{aligned} \tag{15} \]

where \(\lambda+1=p\) and (11) were used.

Divide (15) by (13). Since \(F(r)>0\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{F'(r)}{F(r)} &=-\lambda\frac{a-hr}{hr(1-r)}\\ &=-\lambda \left\{ \frac{a}{hr}-\frac{b}{h(1-r)} \right\}. \end{aligned} \tag{16} \]

To verify the partial fractions in the last line, combine them:

\[ \frac{a}{hr}-\frac{b}{h(1-r)} = \frac{a(1-r)-br}{hr(1-r)} = \frac{a-(a+b)r}{hr(1-r)} = \frac{a-hr}{hr(1-r)}. \]

Part II. Center the ODE solution and obtain a moment transform

Define

\[ r_0=\frac{b}{h}, \qquad c=\frac{ab}{h}, \qquad Z=a-h\theta. \tag{19} \]

The choice \(r_0=b/h\) is important because it makes \(u_\theta(r_0)\) independent of \(\theta\):

\[ \begin{aligned} u_\theta(r_0) &=a\frac bh(1-\theta) +b\left(1-\frac bh\right)\theta\\ &=\frac{ab}{h}(1-\theta) +\frac{ab}{h}\theta\\ &=\frac{ab}{h}=c. \end{aligned} \tag{20} \]

Now write

\[ r=r_0+ct. \tag{21} \]

Since \(u_\theta(r)\) is affine in \(r\), (14) and (20) give

\[ \begin{aligned} u_\theta(r_0+ct) &=u_\theta(r_0)+ct(a-h\theta)\\ &=c(1+tZ). \end{aligned} \tag{22} \]

\[ \begin{aligned} F(r_0+ct) &=c^{-\lambda}E_\nu[(1+tZ)^{-\lambda}],\\ F(r_0) &=E_\nu[c^{-\lambda}] =c^{-\lambda}, \end{aligned} \]

where the last equality uses the fact that \(\nu\) is a probability measure. Dividing these two equalities gives

\[ \frac{F(r_0+ct)}{F(r_0)} =E_\nu[(1+tZ)^{-\lambda}]. \tag{23} \]

On the other hand,

\[ \frac{r_0+ct}{r_0} =1+at, \tag{24} \]

because \(c/r_0=(ab/h)/(b/h)=a\), while

\[ \frac{1-r_0-ct}{1-r_0} =1-bt, \tag{25} \]

because \(c/(1-r_0)=(ab/h)/(a/h)=b\).

\[ \boxed{ E_\nu[(1+tZ)^{-\lambda}] =(1+at)^{-\lambda a/h} (1-bt)^{-\lambda b/h}. } \tag{26} \]

Since \(r=r_0+ct\), the exact interval inherited from \(0<r<1\) is

\[ -\frac1a<t<\frac1b. \]

A shorter annotated elementary proof of Graybill–Deal inadmissibility

The proof has three moves. First, normal regression and one gamma change of variables turn risk comparison into the sign of an integral. Second, a differential identity at \(q=0\) gives a direction in which that integral decreases for every variance ratio. Third, an explicit bump and integration by parts convert this boundary derivative into an ordinary estimator supported strictly inside the observation space. Section 2 spells out this last conversion, which is the conceptual step most easily lost in a condensed proof.

Theorem

Let two independent normal samples have a common unknown mean \(\mu\), positive unknown variances \(\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2\), and sample sizes \(n_1,n_2\ge2\). Under squared-error loss, the Graybill–Deal estimator is inadmissible.

Write \(R(\delta)=E[(\delta-\mu)^2]\) for its risk at a fixed parameter point.

1. Risk and density reduction

Let

\[ S_i^2=\frac1{n_i-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}(X_{ij}-\bar X_i)^2 \]

be the usual unbiased sample variance. Write

\[ a=\frac{n_1-1}{2},\qquad b=\frac{n_2-1}{2},\qquad h=a+b,\qquad p=h+\frac32, \]

\[ v_i=\frac{\sigma_i^2}{n_i},\qquad \tau=v_1+v_2,\qquad \theta=\frac{v_1}{\tau}\in(0,1), \]

and

\[ D=\bar X_2-\bar X_1,\qquad Y_i=\frac{S_i^2}{n_i},\qquad r=\frac{Y_1}{Y_1+Y_2},\qquad q=\frac{D^2}{Y_1+Y_2}. \]

[Annotation] Type, range, and status of every quantity just defined.

The definitions above introduce three different kinds of object. Separating them before anything is proved prevents the commonest confusions later.

(a) Known constants, determined by the sample sizes alone.

\(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are positive integers with \(n_i\ge2\) by hypothesis. They are fixed and known, so everything built from them alone is a known constant.

Since \(n_i\) is an integer, \(n_i-1\) is an integer, and \(n_i\ge2\) gives \(n_i-1\ge1\). Halving an integer is always legitimate, so \(a\) and \(b\) are well-defined expressions, and

\[a=\frac{n_1-1}{2}\ge\frac12,\qquad b=\frac{n_2-1}{2}\ge\frac12 .\]

They are rational — indeed half-integers, \(a,b\in\{\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,2,\dots\}\), with \(a\) an integer exactly when \(n_1\) is odd. They need not be \(\ge1\): \(n_1=2\) gives \(a=\tfrac12\). This is worth recording at the outset, because \(w(r,q)=r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2}\) in (4) then carries the negative power \(r^{-1/2}\) and is unbounded as \(r\downarrow0\). Nothing breaks — \(w\) is only ever used on a compact set interior to \((0,1)\) — but the reader who assumes \(a\ge1\) will be surprised.

Next, \(h=a+b\) is a sum of two rationals, hence rational, and

\[h=\frac{n_1+n_2-2}{2}\ \ge\ \frac12+\frac12=1 .\]

Finally \(p\) is rational, and since \(p=h+\tfrac32\) is an equality,

\[p=h+\frac32=\frac{n_1+n_2+1}{2}\ \ge\ 1+\frac32=\frac52 .\]

So \(p\) is a half-integer, generally not an integer, and \(p\ge\tfrac52>1\). The strict inequality \(p>1\) is exactly what makes the factor \(p-1\) in the certificates (16) and (20) strictly positive, hence both certificate values strictly negative.

Two further consequences are used silently below. First, \(2a=n_1-1\) and \(2b=n_2-1\) are positive integers: they are the chi-square degrees of freedom in §1, and a chi-square law requires at least one degree of freedom, which is precisely \(n_i\ge2\). Second, \(a,b,p>0\), so \(\Gamma(a)\), \(\Gamma(b)\), \(\Gamma(p)\) are all finite and strictly positive and the constant \(C_{a,b}\) is a well-defined positive real.

It is worth noting how much work the hypothesis \(n_i\ge2\) does. It is needed in four separate places: the divisor \(n_i-1\) in \(S_i^2\) must be nonzero; the chi-square law \(\chi^2_{n_i-1}\) needs \(n_i-1\ge1\); \(\Gamma(a)\) needs \(a>0\); and \(p>1\) needs \(h>0\).

(b) Unknown parameters.

\(\mu\in\mathbb R\) is an unrestricted real number — no positivity, no bound. \(\sigma_1^2\) and \(\sigma_2^2\) are strictly positive reals.

Since \(\sigma_i^2\) is a positive real and \(n_i\) is a positive integer, the quotient \(v_i=\sigma_i^2/n_i\) is a well-defined positive real. Hence \(\tau=v_1+v_2\) is a sum of two positive reals and is a positive real. In particular \(\tau\ne0\), so

\[\theta=\frac{v_1}{\tau}\]

is a valid definition — there is no division by zero. Its range is then forced from both sides:

  • \(v_1>0\) and \(\tau>0\) give \(\theta>0\);
  • \(\tau=v_1+v_2>v_1\), because \(v_2>0\), gives \(v_1/\tau<1\), i.e. \(\theta<1\).

So \(0<\theta<1\) strictly, and neither endpoint is attainable by any positive variance pair. Symmetrically \(1-\theta=v_2/\tau\in(0,1)\). The map

\[(v_1,v_2)\longmapsto(\tau,\theta)=\Big(v_1+v_2,\ \frac{v_1}{v_1+v_2}\Big)\]

is a bijection from \((0,\infty)^2\) onto \((0,\infty)\times(0,1)\), with inverse \(v_1=\tau\theta\), \(v_2=\tau(1-\theta)\); so quantifying over all \(\tau>0\) and all \(\theta\in(0,1)\) is exactly quantifying over all positive variance pairs, and no parameter point is lost or double-counted.

(c) Observable random variables.

\(\bar X_1,\bar X_2\) and \(D=\bar X_2-\bar X_1\) are real-valued random variables taking any real value. \(D\) has a continuous (normal) law, so \(\mathbb P(D=0)=0\).

\(S_i^2\) is a sum of squares divided by a positive integer, so \(S_i^2\ge0\) always, and \(S_i^2>0\) almost surely. Hence \(Y_i=S_i^2/n_i\ge0\) always and \(Y_i>0\) almost surely, so \(Y_1+Y_2>0\) almost surely and both \(r\) and \(q\) are well-defined off a null event. On that event \(0<r<1\) and \(q\ge0\), with \(q>0\) almost surely since \(\mathbb P(D=0)=0\); the value \(q=0\) is nevertheless the boundary point at which the certificates are evaluated.

Crucially, \(r\) and \(q\) are statistics: they are functions of the data alone and contain no unknown parameter. This is what makes \(\bar X_1+D\phi(r,q)\) an estimator rather than an oracle.

(d) A caution about \(\theta\) versus \(r\). Both lie in \((0,1)\), but they are different kinds of object: \(\theta\) is an unknown parameter, \(r\) is an observable statistic. From (7) onward \(\theta\) acquires a third role as a free real variable ranging over the closed interval \([0,1]\) inside the functions \(B_\theta\) and \(K_\theta\); there the endpoints are admitted purely to make the parameter set compact and carry no statistical meaning.

which vanishes exactly when \(\theta=v_1/\tau\) — the definition of \(\theta\).

This is the only place the choice of \(\theta\) is used, and it is no accident. The condition \(\theta v_2=(1-\theta)v_1\) just derived is the stationarity condition of

\[\operatorname{Var}\big((1-\theta)\bar X_1+\theta\bar X_2\big) =(1-\theta)^2v_1+\theta^2v_2,\]

whose derivative in \(\theta\) is \(2\big(\theta v_2-(1-\theta)v_1\big)\). So \(\theta\) is the variance-minimising — that is, inverse-variance, or “oracle” — weight, and \(\operatorname{Cov}(E,D)=0\) is precisely its first-order condition. It is the population version of the quantity Graybill–Deal estimates by \(r\).

(iii) \(E\) and \(D\) are independent. This needs care, since joint normality does not follow from normality of the marginals, and both \(E\) and \(D\) are built from both samples.

(iii-a) \((\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\) is jointly normal. By Lemma B.1 in the appendix, a random vector \(W\) is multivariate normal precisely when \(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W\) is univariate normal for every fixed \(\alpha\). For any \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) the variable \(\alpha_1\bar X_1+\alpha_2\bar X_2\) is a sum of two independent normal variables, hence normal by Lemma B.3. So

\[(\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\sim N_2\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mu\\\mu\end{pmatrix},\ \Sigma\right), \qquad \Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(v_1,v_2).\]

This is the step that uses independence of the two samples, and it is exactly what marginal normality alone would fail to give.

(iii-b) \((E,D)\) is jointly normal. By (A.1) both coordinates are affine functions of \((\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\), so any linear combination of them is again such an affine function, hence normal by (iii-a); apply Lemma B.1 again. Explicitly,

\[\begin{pmatrix}E\\ D\end{pmatrix} =A\begin{pmatrix}\bar X_1\\ \bar X_2\end{pmatrix} +\begin{pmatrix}-\mu\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}1-\theta & \theta\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix},\]

so by Lemma B.2 the covariance matrix of \((E,D)\) is \(A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}\). Carrying out the multiplication with \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(v_1,v_2)\),

\[A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}= \begin{pmatrix} (1-\theta)^2v_1+\theta^2v_2 & -(1-\theta)v_1+\theta v_2\\[2mm] -(1-\theta)v_1+\theta v_2 & v_1+v_2 \end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\tau\theta(1-\theta) & 0\\[2mm] 0 & \tau\end{pmatrix}. \tag{A.2}\]

The vanishing off-diagonal entry is the computation of (ii) again. The lower-right entry proves the assertion \(\operatorname{Var}(D)=\tau\) used throughout; since \(D\) is normal with mean \(0\), it follows that \(D_0=D/\sqrt\tau\) is standard normal and hence \(Z=D_0^2\sim\chi^2_1\), as used in the density calculation. The upper-left entry is the stated \(\operatorname{Var}(E)=\tau\theta(1-\theta)\); it does not involve the weight, which is why comparing two estimators of the form \(\bar X_1+D\phi\) reduces to comparing \(\mathbb E[D^2(\phi-\theta)^2]\).

(iii-c) Zero covariance now gives independence. Both diagonal entries of (A.2) are strictly positive because \(v_1,v_2>0\) and \(0<\theta<1\), so the covariance matrix \(\Lambda\) of \((E,D)\) is nonsingular and the pair has the Gaussian density \((2\pi)^{-1}(\det\Lambda)^{-1/2}\exp\big(-\tfrac12 y^{\mathsf T}\Lambda^{-1}y\big)\), the mean vector being \((0,0)^{\mathsf T}\) by (i) and by \(\mathbb E D=\mu-\mu=0\). Since \(\Lambda\) is diagonal, so is \(\Lambda^{-1}\), and the quadratic form splits as

\[y^{\mathsf T}\Lambda^{-1}y =y_1\Lambda^{-1}_{1,1}y_1+y_2\Lambda^{-1}_{2,2}y_2 .\]

The exponential of a sum is a product, and because \(\Lambda\) is diagonal \(\det\Lambda=\Lambda_{1,1}\Lambda_{2,2}\) and \(\Lambda^{-1}_{i,i}=1/\Lambda_{i,i}\), so the constant splits along with the exponent and the two factors are visibly the \(N(0,\Lambda_{1,1})\) and \(N(0,\Lambda_{2,2})\) densities:

\[f(y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\Lambda_{1,1}\Lambda_{2,2}}} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{y_1^2}{\Lambda_{1,1}} +\tfrac{y_2^2}{\Lambda_{2,2}}\Big)\right) =\left[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Lambda_{1,1}}}e^{-y_1^2/(2\Lambda_{1,1})}\right] \left[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Lambda_{2,2}}}e^{-y_2^2/(2\Lambda_{2,2})}\right].\]

Since the joint density is the product of the two marginal densities, \(E\perp D\).

(iv) \((\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\) is independent of \((S_1^2,S_2^2)\). Within a normal sample the sample mean is independent of the vector of residuals, hence of \(S_i^2\); and the two samples are independent of each other.

This deduction needs care. It is not enough that \(\bar X_1\perp S_1^2\) and \(\bar X_2\perp S_2^2\): in general \(A\perp B\) and \(C\perp D\) do not give \((A,C)\perp(B,D)\). (Take \(A\perp B\) and set \(C=B\), \(D=A\); then \(C\perp D\), yet \((A,C)\) and \((B,D)\) determine each other.) Here is the argument in full. Pick any sets and consider the four events

\[E_1=\{\bar X_1\in A_1\},\quad F_1=\{S_1^2\in B_1\},\quad E_2=\{\bar X_2\in A_2\},\quad F_2=\{S_2^2\in B_2\}.\]

Step 1 (the two samples are independent). \(E_1\cap F_1\) is determined by the observations of sample 1 alone, and \(E_2\cap F_2\) by those of sample 2 alone, so

\[\mathbb P(E_1\cap F_1\cap E_2\cap F_2) =\mathbb P(E_1\cap F_1)\,\mathbb P(E_2\cap F_2).\]

Step 2 (within each sample). Since \(\bar X_i\perp S_i^2\), \(\mathbb P(E_i\cap F_i)=\mathbb P(E_i)\mathbb P(F_i)\) for \(i=1,2\). Substituting,

\[\mathbb P(E_1\cap F_1\cap E_2\cap F_2) =\mathbb P(E_1)\,\mathbb P(F_1)\,\mathbb P(E_2)\,\mathbb P(F_2).\]

Step 3 (regroup). Note \(\bar X_1\perp\bar X_2\) and \(S_1^2\perp S_2^2\), since the samples are independent. Grouping the two means together and the two variances together,

\[=\big[\mathbb P(E_1)\mathbb P(E_2)\big]\big[\mathbb P(F_1)\mathbb P(F_2)\big] =\mathbb P(E_1\cap E_2)\,\mathbb P(F_1\cap F_2).\]

This says exactly that \((\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\perp(S_1^2,S_2^2)\). The point is Step 3: the within-sample independences alone give only the two brackets \(\mathbb P(E_i\cap F_i)=\mathbb P(E_i)\mathbb P(F_i)\), which pair each mean with its own variance. It is the cross-sample independence that breaks the product all the way down into four separate factors, after which regrouping means-with-means is legitimate.

(v) \(E\) is independent of the triple \((D,Y_1,Y_2)\). Since \(Y_i=S_i^2/n_i\) is a function of \(S_i^2\), and \((E,D)\) is a function of \((\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\), step (iv) gives

\[(E,D)\ \perp\ (Y_1,Y_2).\]

Now the same factor-then-regroup argument as in (iv). Write \(Y=(Y_1,Y_2)\) and take any sets \(A,B,C\) for \(E,D,Y\) respectively. Then

\[\mathbb P(E\in A,\ D\in B,\ Y\in C) =\mathbb P\big((E,D)\in A\times B\big)\ \mathbb P(Y\in C)\]

by the display above, and applying \(E\perp D\) from (iii) to the first factor,

\[=\mathbb P(E\in A)\ \mathbb P(D\in B)\ \mathbb P(Y\in C).\]

The probability is now a product of three separate factors, so regroup the last two, using \(D\perp Y\) — which is free, being the display above restricted to the second coordinate:

\[=\mathbb P(E\in A)\ \mathbb P(D\in B,\ Y\in C).\]

The result is exactly \(E\perp(D,Y_1,Y_2)\) on product sets, and hence in general.

Conclusion. For any weight \(\phi=\phi(r,q)\), the variable \(D(\phi-\theta)\) is a function of \((D,Y_1,Y_2)\), since \(r\) and \(q\) are. By (v) and (i) the cross term factorises,

\[\mathbb E\big[E\,D(\phi-\theta)\big] =\mathbb E[E]\cdot\mathbb E\big[D(\phi-\theta)\big]=0,\]

which is the assertion used in the next display. Only \(\mathbb E E=0\) and independence were used, so no assumption on \(\phi\) beyond integrability of the displayed terms is needed. \(\square\)

Consequently, for any bounded measurable \(H(r,q)\), the estimator

\[ \delta_\varepsilon =\bar X_1+D\{r+\varepsilon H(r,q)\} \]

satisfies

\[ \delta_\varepsilon-\mu =E+D\{r-\theta+\varepsilon H(r,q)\}. \]

The cross term with \(E\) has expectation zero, so subtracting the case \(\varepsilon=0\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} R(\delta_\varepsilon)-R(\delta_{\rm GD}) &=E\!\left[D^2\left\{ 2\varepsilon H(r,q)(r-\theta)+\varepsilon^2H(r,q)^2 \right\}\right]. \end{aligned} \tag{2} \]

Here and below the risk is at the fixed parameter point \((\mu,\tau,\theta)\).

[Annotation] Derivation of (2). By the decomposition just proved, applied to each of the two estimators separately,

\[R(\delta_{\rm GD}) =\mathbb E[E^2]+\mathbb E\big[D^2(r-\theta)^2\big],\] \[R(\delta_\varepsilon) =\mathbb E[E^2]+\mathbb E\big[D^2(r+\varepsilon H-\theta)^2\big].\]

The term \(\mathbb E[E^2]\) is the same in both — it does not involve the weight — so it cancels in the difference:

\[R(\delta_\varepsilon)-R(\delta_{\rm GD}) =\mathbb E\Big[D^2\big\{(r-\theta+\varepsilon H)^2-(r-\theta)^2\big\}\Big].\]

Expanding the square by the elementary identity \((\text{first}+\text{second})^2-(\text{first})^2 =2(\text{first})(\text{second})+(\text{second})^2\), with first term \(r-\theta\) and second term \(\varepsilon H\),

\[(r-\theta+\varepsilon H)^2-(r-\theta)^2 =2\varepsilon(r-\theta)H+\varepsilon^2H^2 .\]

Multiplying by \(D^2\) and taking expectations gives (2). Note this is an exact identity, not an approximation: no expansion in small \(\varepsilon\) has been made, and the only property of \(H\) used is that \(r+\varepsilon H\) is again a function of \((D,Y_1,Y_2)\), so that the decomposition applies to it. (The dummy names in this expansion are deliberately not \(a,b\), which are reserved throughout for the gamma shapes.)

We next calculate the measure in (2). Put

\[ G_i=\frac{Y_i}{\tau},\qquad D_0=\frac D{\sqrt\tau},\qquad Z=D_0^2. \]

The three variables are independent, with

\[ G_1/\theta\sim\frac{\chi^2_{2a}}{2a},\qquad G_2/(1-\theta)\sim\frac{\chi^2_{2b}}{2b},\qquad Z\sim\chi^2_1. \]

[Annotation] The three laws and their independence.

The laws. Since \(2a=n_1-1\), \(Y_1=S_1^2/n_1\), and \(v_1=\sigma_1^2/n_1\), the factor \(n_1\) cancels:

\[\frac{Y_1}{v_1}=\frac{S_1^2/n_1}{\sigma_1^2/n_1}=\frac{S_1^2}{\sigma_1^2} =\frac1{2a}\cdot\frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2}{\sigma_1^2}\sim\frac{\chi^2_{2a}}{2a},\]

using the standard normal-sample fact \((n_i-1)S_i^2/\sigma_i^2\sim\chi^2_{n_i-1}\). Because \(G_1=Y_1/\tau=(v_1/\tau)(Y_1/v_1)=\theta\,(Y_1/v_1)\), this is exactly the first assertion; the second follows identically with \(2b=n_2-1\) and \(v_2/\tau=1-\theta\). For the third, (A.2) gave \(\operatorname{Var}(D)=\tau\), so \(D_0=D/\sqrt\tau\sim N(0,1)\) and \(Z=D_0^2\sim\chi^2_1\).

A chi-square is a gamma. In the shape–rate parametrisation the gamma density with shape \(\alpha>0\) and rate \(\beta>0\) is

\[f_{\operatorname{Gamma}}(x;\alpha,\beta) =\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}, \qquad x>0,\]

while the \(\chi^2_k\) density is \(\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\Gamma(k/2)}x^{k/2-1}e^{-x/2}\). Putting \(\alpha=k/2\) and \(\beta=1/2\) in the first reproduces the second, so \(\chi^2_k=\operatorname{Gamma}(k/2,\ \text{rate }1/2)\). Applying the scaling above, \(G_1\) is \(\operatorname{Gamma}(a,\ \text{rate }a/\theta)\) and \(G_2\) is \(\operatorname{Gamma}(b,\ \text{rate }b/(1-\theta))\), which is what produces the two exponential terms in (3).

Independence. Step (iv) of the oracle decomposition proved \((\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\perp(S_1^2,S_2^2)\). Now \(D\), and hence \(Z=D^2/\tau\), is a function of the sample means, while \(G_1,G_2\) are functions of the sample variances; applying functions to two independent random vectors preserves their independence, so \(Z\perp(G_1,G_2)\). Separately \(G_1\perp G_2\), since the two samples are independent. Together these make \(G_1,G_2,Z\) mutually independent, which is what licenses multiplying the three marginal densities in (3).

A contrast worth noting. In the equal-sample-size argument of SHORT_PROOF_N17_ANNOTATED.md, the two gamma variables share the same rate \(1/2\), the \(p\)-dependence cancels out of the exponential, and the beta–gamma factorisation makes the ratio independent of the total. Here the rates \(a/\theta\) and \(b/(1-\theta)\) differ, so no such cancellation occurs: the scale \(s\) stays coupled to \(r\) and \(q\) through \(B_\theta\) in the exponent. That coupling is precisely what survives the \(s\)-integration as the factor \(B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}\) in (5), and it is the reason the kernel \(K_\theta\) has any \(r\)-dependence to differentiate at all.

After multiplication by the risk weight \(Z\), their joint density, apart from a positive constant depending only on \(a,b\), is

\[ \theta^{-a}(1-\theta)^{-b} G_1^{a-1}G_2^{b-1}Z^{1/2} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{aG_1}{\theta} -\frac{bG_2}{1-\theta}-\frac Z2\right\}. \tag{3} \]

Make the change of variables

\[ G_1=sr,\qquad G_2=s(1-r),\qquad Z=sq,\qquad s>0. \]

Its Jacobian is \(s^2\). If

\[ \begin{aligned} B_\theta(r,q) &=2ar(1-\theta)+2b(1-r)\theta+q\theta(1-\theta),\\ w(r,q)&=r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}q^{1/2}, \end{aligned} \tag{4} \]

then the power of \(s\) in (3) is \(s^{p-1}\), and its exponential rate is \(B_\theta/\{2\theta(1-\theta)\}\). Thus

\[ \int_0^\infty s^{p-1} e^{-sB_\theta/\{2\theta(1-\theta)\}}\,ds =\Gamma(p)\left\{\frac{2\theta(1-\theta)}{B_\theta}\right\}^{p}. \]

It follows that, for every integrable \(F\),

\[ \begin{aligned} E_\theta[D_0^2F(r,q)] ={}&C_{a,b}\theta^{\,b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2}\\ &\quad{}\times \int F(r,q)B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}w(r,q)\,dr\,dq, \end{aligned} \tag{5} \]

where

\[ C_{a,b}= \frac{2^p a^a b^b\Gamma(p)} {\sqrt{2\pi}\,\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}>0. \]

Its value is irrelevant to the sign, but displaying it makes the density calculation fully checkable. Thus (5) proves the reduced density rather than merely quoting it.

Equation (5) is used as a density formula only for the actual parameter range \(0<\theta<1\). We later let \(\theta=0,1\) only in the factored kernel \(B_\theta^{-p}\), where the extension is continuous and is useful for compactness.

2. The elementary localization lemma

Define the first-variation kernel

\[ K_\theta(r,q)=(r-\theta)B_\theta(r,q)^{-p}. \tag{6} \]

To see the strategy before the details, insert (5) into (2). Apart from a positive factor, the term linear in \(\varepsilon\) is \(2\varepsilon\int HK_\theta w\); the quadratic cost is \(\varepsilon^2\int H^2B_\theta^{-p}w\). Thus a single \(H\), independent of the unknown \(\theta\), whose linear integral is uniformly negative will win for all sufficiently small \(\varepsilon\). The lemma constructs exactly such an \(H\).

The following lemma turns one finite differential calculation into a dominating estimator.

Local certificate lemma. Suppose \(r_*\in(0,1)\), \(L\) is a constant-coefficient differential operator of order at most three, and

\[ (LK_\theta)(r_*,0)=-\kappa\qquad(0\le\theta\le1) \tag{7} \]

for some \(\kappa>0\). Then there is a bounded, compactly supported, parameter-free \(H\) for which

\[ \sup_{0\le\theta\le1} \int H(r,q)K_\theta(r,q)w(r,q)\,dr\,dq<0. \tag{8} \]

Moreover, \(\bar X_1+D\{r+\varepsilon H(r,q)\}\) strictly dominates Graybill–Deal for all sufficiently small fixed \(\varepsilon>0\).

Proof. When \(r\) stays in a compact subinterval of \((0,1)\) and \(q\ge0\),

\[ B_\theta(r,q)\ge\min\{2ar,\,2b(1-r)\}>0 \tag{9} \]

uniformly for \(0\le\theta\le1\). Hence \(LK_\theta\) is jointly continuous in \((\theta,r,q)\) near the compact line \(\{(\theta,r_*,0):0\le\theta\le1\}\). By (7) and uniform continuity, there is an open rectangle \(U\Subset(0,1)\times(0,\infty)\), lying arbitrarily close to \((r_*,0)\), on which

\[ (LK_\theta)(r,q)\le-\kappa/2 \quad\text{for every }(r,q)\in U,\ 0\le\theta\le1. \tag{10} \]

Choose \(r_c,q_c,\alpha,\beta\) so that the closed rectangle \(\{|r-r_c|\le\alpha,\ |q-q_c|\le\beta\}\) lies in \(U\), and put

\[ \rho(r,q)= \left[1-\left(\frac{r-r_c}{\alpha}\right)^2\right]_+^4 \left[1-\left(\frac{q-q_c}{\beta}\right)^2\right]_+^4. \tag{11} \]

This nonzero bump is \(C^3\), nonnegative, and compactly supported in \(U\): each factor has a zero of order four at both endpoints. If

\[ L=\sum c_{ij}\partial_r^i\partial_q^j,\qquad L^*=\sum(-1)^{i+j}c_{ij}\partial_r^i\partial_q^j, \]

define

\[ H=\frac{L^*\rho}{w} \]

on \(U\), and \(H=0\) elsewhere. Since \(w>0\) on \(\overline U\), \(H\) is bounded and compactly supported; \(L^*\rho\) already vanishes on a neighborhood of \(\partial U\), so this zero extension causes no seam. The point of the adjoint is to move the derivatives in \(L^*\rho\) back onto \(K_\theta\). Repeated integration by parts has no boundary terms and gives, uniformly in \(\theta\),

\[ \begin{aligned} I_\theta &:=\int HK_\theta w =\int(L^*\rho)K_\theta =\int\rho(LK_\theta)\\ &\le-\frac{\kappa}{2}\int\rho=:-\gamma<0. \end{aligned} \tag{12} \]

Also

\[ J_\theta:=\int H^2B_\theta^{-p}w,\qquad M:=\sup_{0\le\theta\le1}J_\theta \]

satisfies \(0<M<\infty\): all factors are bounded on the compact set \([0,1]\times\operatorname{supp}H\), and (12) implies \(H\not\equiv0\). Combining (2), (5), and (12), we obtain

\[ \begin{aligned} R(\delta_\varepsilon)-R(\delta_{\rm GD}) &\le \tau C_{a,b}\theta^{b+3/2}(1-\theta)^{a+3/2} (-2\varepsilon\gamma+\varepsilon^2M). \end{aligned} \tag{13} \]

For \(0<\varepsilon<2\gamma/M\), this is strictly negative whenever \(\tau>0\) and \(0<\theta<1\). The original rule has finite risk because \(0<r<1\), and the correction does too, since

\[ E[(DH)^2]\le\lVert H\rVert_\infty^2E[D^2] =\tau\lVert H\rVert_\infty^2<\infty. \]

All choices depend only on the known sample sizes. \(\square\)

Thus it remains only to produce (7).

3. Two short certificate checks

For fixed \(\theta\), set

\[ x=r-\theta,\qquad A=\partial_rB_\theta=2(a-h\theta),\qquad Q=\partial_qB_\theta=\theta(1-\theta). \]

Because \(B_\theta\) is affine in \(r,q\), differentiating \(K=xB^{-p}\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} K_r&=B^{-p}-pxAB^{-p-1},\\ K_{rr}&=-2pAB^{-p-1}+p(p+1)xA^2B^{-p-2},\\ K_q&=-pxQB^{-p-1},\\ K_{rq}&=-pQB^{-p-1}+p(p+1)xAQB^{-p-2},\\ K_{rrr}&=3p(p+1)A^2B^{-p-2} -p(p+1)(p+2)xA^3B^{-p-3}. \end{aligned} \tag{14} \]

These are all the derivatives used below.

The pivot in each case is chosen so that \(B_\theta(r_*,0)\) no longer depends on \(\theta\). The operators may look engineered, but nothing is being invoked about them: equations (17)–(22) verify directly that their action is one fixed negative number. Multiplying an operator by a positive constant does not affect the argument, so the versions below are rescaled to remove most denominators.

Unequal sample sizes

Suppose \(a\ne b\), and set

\[ \Delta=a-b,\qquad r_0=\frac bh,\qquad c=\frac{2ab}{h}. \]

Use the rescaled operator

\[ L_u=\operatorname{sgn}(\Delta)\left[ -p(p-1)h+(p+1)\Delta\,\partial_r +\frac{ab}{h}\,\partial_r^2-2(p+1)h^2\,\partial_q \right]. \tag{15} \]

We claim

\[ (L_uK_\theta)(r_0,0) =-c^{-p}(p-1)|\Delta|<0. \tag{16} \]

Here is the complete check. At \((r_0,0)\), retain \(x=r_0-\theta\) and put \(u=a-h\theta=\Delta+hx\). Then

\[ B=c,\qquad A=2u,\qquad Q=\theta(1-\theta), \]

and direct expansion gives the useful identity

\[ \begin{aligned} u^2+h^2Q-\Delta u &=(a-h\theta)^2+h^2\theta(1-\theta) -(a-b)(a-h\theta)\\ &=ab. \end{aligned} \tag{17} \]

Let \(M_u\) denote the operator inside the brackets in (15). Substitution in (14), using \(c=2ab/h\), first gives

\[ \begin{aligned} c^pK&=x,& c^pK_r&=1-\frac{phxu}{ab},\\ c^pK_{rr}&=-\frac{2phu}{ab} +\frac{p(p+1)h^2xu^2}{a^2b^2},& c^pK_q&=-\frac{phxQ}{2ab}. \end{aligned} \]

Applying \(M_u\) and grouping \(u^2,Q,\Delta u\) now gives

\[ \begin{aligned} c^p(M_uK_\theta)(r_0,0) ={}&-p(p-1)hx+(p+1)\Delta-2pu\\ &+\frac{p(p+1)hx}{ab}\{u^2+h^2Q-\Delta u\}\\ ={}&-p(p-1)hx+(p+1)\Delta-2p(\Delta+hx) +p(p+1)hx\\ =&-(p-1)\Delta. \end{aligned} \tag{18} \]

Multiplication by \(\operatorname{sgn}(\Delta)c^{-p}\) proves (16). This replaces the cubic coefficient table in the longer proof.

Equal sample sizes

Suppose \(a=b\), put \(d=p^2+p+1\), and use

\[ L_e=4d\,\partial_r+16a(p+2)\,\partial_r\partial_q-\partial_r^3. \tag{19} \]

We claim

\[ (L_eK_\theta)(1/2,0)=-4a^{-p}(p-1)<0. \tag{20} \]

For the check, put \(x=1/2-\theta\) and \(y=4x^2\). At \((r,q)=(1/2,0)\),

\[ B=a,\qquad A=4ax,\qquad Q=\frac{1-y}{4}. \]

Formula (14) therefore becomes

\[ \begin{aligned} a^pK_r&=1-py,\\ a^pK_{rq}&=\frac{p}{4a}(1-y)\{(p+1)y-1\},\\ a^pK_{rrr}&=4p(p+1)y\{3-(p+2)y\}. \end{aligned} \tag{21} \]

Consequently

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{a^p}{4}(L_eK_\theta)(1/2,0) ={}&d(1-py) +p(p+2)(1-y)\{(p+1)y-1\}\\ &-p(p+1)y\{3-(p+2)y\}. \end{aligned} \tag{22} \]

The last two terms have cancelling \(y^2\)-coefficients and equal

\[ \begin{aligned} p\{-(p+2)+[(p+2)^2-3(p+1)]y\} &=-p(p+2)+pdy. \end{aligned} \]

Thus (22) is

\[ d(1-py)-p(p+2)+pdy=d-p(p+2)=1-p, \]

which proves (20).

Since \(a,b\ge1/2\), we have \(p=h+3/2\ge5/2>1\). Therefore (16) or (20), according as \(a\ne b\) or \(a=b\), supplies the hypothesis of the local certificate lemma. The estimator constructed there has strictly smaller risk for every \(\mu\in\mathbb R\) and every positive variance pair, proving the theorem. \(\square\)

Optional legitimacy checks

The construction already proves inadmissibility, but two extra properties cost almost nothing to verify.

First, conditional on \((Y_1,Y_2)\), \(H(r,q)\) depends on the centered normal contrast \(D\) only through \(D^2\). Hence \(DH(r,q)\) is odd in \(D\), so the perturbation remains unbiased.

Second, \(\operatorname{supp}H\) stays a positive distance \(d_0\) from \(r=0,1\). Shrinking \(\varepsilon\) further so that \(\varepsilon\lVert H\rVert_\infty<d_0\) keeps \(0\le r+\varepsilon H(r,q)\le1\). Thus the improved rule may even be kept as a convex combination of the two sample means.

[Annotation] An alternative route to a weight in \([0,1]\). Shrinking \(\varepsilon\) is not the only option. As in SHORT_PROOF_N17_ANNOTATED.md, one may instead replace the weight \(\phi_\varepsilon=r+\varepsilon H\) by its projection onto \([0,1]\),

\[\phi_\varepsilon^{\rm clip} =\min\{1,\max\{0,\phi_\varepsilon\}\}.\]

Since \(0<\theta<1\), projecting onto \([0,1]\) cannot increase the distance to \(\theta\): for any real \(t\), the projected value is at least as close to \(\theta\) as \(t\) is, so

\[(\phi_\varepsilon^{\rm clip}-\theta)^2\le(\phi_\varepsilon-\theta)^2\]

pointwise. By the decomposition, the risk of \(\bar X_1+D\phi\) is \(\mathbb E[E^2]+\mathbb E[D^2(\phi-\theta)^2]\), which is monotone in that pointwise quantity, so the clipped rule has risk no larger than \(\delta_\varepsilon\) and therefore also strictly dominates Graybill–Deal. This route needs no bound on \(\lVert H\rVert_\infty\) at all, but it gives up the unbiasedness of the first check, since clipping is not odd in \(D\).

Appendix B. The Gaussian facts used in the oracle decomposition

[Annotation] This appendix is not part of the original proof. It supplies the three standard facts invoked in step (iii) of the oracle decomposition, transferred unchanged from SHORT_PROOF_N17_ANNOTATED.md; nothing in them depends on the sample sizes, so they apply verbatim here.

They are proved with characteristic functions rather than moment generating functions. The reason is rigour: \(\varphi_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{itX}]\) exists for every random variable, since \(|e^{itX}|=1\), and the uniqueness theorem — two probability measures on \(\mathbb R^k\) with the same characteristic function coincide — holds without side conditions. A moment generating function may be infinite off the origin, and MGF-based uniqueness requires finiteness on a neighbourhood of \(0\). For the Gaussians here that does hold, so an MGF argument is also valid; but it needs a hypothesis the CF argument does not.

Conventions. Call \(X\) univariate normal \(N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), allowing the degenerate case \(\sigma^2=0\) (a point mass at \(\mu\)), if \(\varphi_X(t)=\exp(it\mu-\tfrac12t^2\sigma^2)\) for all \(t\in\mathbb R\). Call \(W\in\mathbb R^k\) multivariate normal \(N_k(m,\Sigma)\), with \(\Sigma\) symmetric nonnegative-definite, if

\[ \varphi_W(t)=\mathbb E\big[e^{it^{\mathsf T}W}\big] =\exp\big(it^{\mathsf T}m-\tfrac12t^{\mathsf T}\Sigma t\big), \qquad t\in\mathbb R^k. \tag{B.1} \]

Admitting degenerate cases is not cosmetic: a linear combination \(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W\) can have zero variance, and Lemma B.1 would be false if such combinations were not counted as normal.

Lemma B.1 (Cramér–Wold characterisation)

\(W\in\mathbb R^k\) is multivariate normal if and only if \(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W\) is univariate normal for every \(\alpha\in\mathbb R^k\).

Proof. (\(\Rightarrow\)) Assume (B.1) and fix \(\alpha\). For \(t\in\mathbb R\),

\[ \varphi_{\alpha^{\mathsf T}W}(t) =\mathbb E\big[e^{i(t\alpha)^{\mathsf T}W}\big] =\varphi_W(t\alpha) =\exp\big(it\,\alpha^{\mathsf T}m-\tfrac12t^2\,\alpha^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\alpha\big), \]

the characteristic function of \(N(\alpha^{\mathsf T}m,\alpha^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\alpha)\), the variance being \(\ge0\) since \(\Sigma\) is nonnegative-definite.

(\(\Leftarrow\)) Assume every \(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W\) is univariate normal. Taking \(\alpha=e_j\) shows each \(W_j\) is normal, hence has finite mean and variance; by Cauchy–Schwarz all covariances are finite. So \(m:=\mathbb E W\) and \(\Sigma:=\operatorname{Cov}(W)\) exist, \(\Sigma\) is symmetric nonnegative-definite, and by linearity of expectation and bilinearity of covariance — neither of which needs normality —

\[ \mathbb E[\alpha^{\mathsf T}W]=\alpha^{\mathsf T}m, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W)=\alpha^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\alpha . \]

By hypothesis \(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W\) is normal, and a univariate normal law is determined by its mean and variance, so \(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W\sim N(\alpha^{\mathsf T}m,\alpha^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\alpha)\) and

\[ \mathbb E\big[e^{it(\alpha^{\mathsf T}W)}\big] =\exp\big(it\,\alpha^{\mathsf T}m-\tfrac12t^2\,\alpha^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\alpha\big). \]

Evaluate at \(t=1\): the left side is exactly \(\varphi_W(\alpha)\), so

\[ \varphi_W(\alpha)=\exp\big(i\alpha^{\mathsf T}m-\tfrac12\alpha^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\alpha\big) \qquad\text{for every }\alpha\in\mathbb R^k, \]

which is (B.1). Uniqueness of characteristic functions finishes. \(\square\)

The step worth noting is the last one: a one-parameter family of univariate characteristic functions, evaluated at the single point \(t=1\) but allowed to range over all directions \(\alpha\), recovers the whole \(k\)-dimensional characteristic function. This is the Cramér–Wold device.

Lemma B.2 (affine images)

If \(W\sim N_k(m,\Sigma)\), \(A\) is \(\ell\times k\) and \(b\in\mathbb R^\ell\), then \(AW+b\sim N_\ell(Am+b,\ A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T})\).

Proof. For \(t\in\mathbb R^\ell\),

\[ \varphi_{AW+b}(t)=e^{it^{\mathsf T}b}\varphi_W(A^{\mathsf T}t) =\exp\big(it^{\mathsf T}(Am+b)-\tfrac12t^{\mathsf T}(A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T})t\big), \]

using \((A^{\mathsf T}t)^{\mathsf T}m=t^{\mathsf T}Am\) and \((A^{\mathsf T}t)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma(A^{\mathsf T}t)=t^{\mathsf T}A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}t\). Also \(A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}\) is nonnegative-definite, since \(t^{\mathsf T}A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}t=(A^{\mathsf T}t)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma(A^{\mathsf T}t)\ge0\). Uniqueness gives the claim. \(\square\)

This is what (iii-b) uses, with the matrix \(A\) there and \(b=(-\mu,0)^{\mathsf T}\); it delivers (A.2) directly as \(A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}\).

Lemma B.3 (sums of independent normals)

If \(X\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\) and \(Y\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\) are independent and \(c_1,c_2\in\mathbb R\), then \(c_1X+c_2Y\sim N(c_1\mu_1+c_2\mu_2,\ c_1^2\sigma_1^2+c_2^2\sigma_2^2)\).

Proof. Independence makes the characteristic function of a sum the product of the characteristic functions, so

\[ \varphi_{c_1X+c_2Y}(t)=\varphi_X(c_1t)\varphi_Y(c_2t) =\exp\Big(it(c_1\mu_1+c_2\mu_2) -\tfrac12t^2\big(c_1^2\sigma_1^2+c_2^2\sigma_2^2\big)\Big). \ \square \]

The proof shows exactly where independence enters: it is what turns the characteristic function of the sum into a product, so that the two Gaussian exponents add. Without it the product formula fails and the sum need not be normal — which is why joint normality of \((\bar X_1,\bar X_2)\) is a consequence of the samples being independent, not of the marginals being normal.

[Annotation] The coefficient letters in Lemma B.3 are \(c_1,c_2\) rather than the \(a,b\) of the source document, since \(a\) and \(b\) are the gamma shapes everywhere else in this proof.
